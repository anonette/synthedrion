# Rehearsing Sovereignty: A Synthetic Roundtable as an Instrument for Studying Performed Authority in the AI Cold War

*Working draft. Co-authored. See ARGUMENT.md for the conceptual scaffold and the

em-dashes. Bracketed notes mark what each co-author is best placed to write.*

**Authors (provisional).** Denisa Reshef Kera, Eliyahu Keller, Avi Spitz,
Oluwakorede Ajibona, James Usher, Lena Slachmuijlder, Merav Turgeman, Hila Ofek.

## Abstract (draft)

The rivalry over artificial intelligence is routinely narrated as a race between
the United States and China for the best models and the most compute. A growing
body of work argues instead that authority over AI is increasingly performed
through signals, imaginaries, and infrastructural promises rather than secured
through enforceable control, a condition we have elsewhere called
meta-sovereignty. That account, however, can only read the performance
backwards, from the announcements and pledges states have already made. This
article introduces an instrument for reading it forwards. Synthēdrion is a
human-AI roundtable in which language-model agents argue as rival powers from
distinct policy corpora while a room of humans interrupts, reprompts, and
summons challenger agents to break any settling consensus. We treat this
apparatus not as a forecasting device but as a synthetic rehearsal: a way to
externalize the maintenance work of performed sovereignty and to observe where
that performance is sustained, repaired, and broken under live contestation. We
specify the method, its controls, and its built-in limit, that the models are
trained on the very discourses the roundtable examines, and we show what running
the rehearsal makes visible that a retrospective reading cannot. The
contribution is a method that makes performed sovereignty observable and
stress-testable, and a sharpening of meta-sovereignty from a description of what
states do into an account of the labor by which they keep doing it.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant story about artificial intelligence and geopolitics is a race. Two
powers, the United States and China, are said to be sprinting for the best
frontier models, the largest compute clusters, and the tightest grip on the
supply of advanced chips, with everyone else strung out behind. The race framing
is intuitive, it fits an older Cold War script, and it is increasingly too
narrow to hold what is actually happening. It assumes that the nation-state is
the unit of power, that winning means possessing the best domestic system, and
that authority follows capability. None of these assumptions survives contact
with the present arrangement, in which a state can announce sovereign AI while
renting its models, its cloud, and its energy from a rival, and in which the
promise of access to chips can be offered at a negotiating table like a security
guarantee. [Denisa: open with a concrete 2025-26 vignette from the ledger of
compute-as-statecraft, e.g. the reported use of Nvidia-chip access in the
Armenia-Azerbaijan diplomacy, and the sovereign-AI wave that leaves most of its
184 national projects dependent on foreign layers.]

We have argued elsewhere that what the race framing misses is a shift in the
mode of authority itself. Governments increasingly rule AI not through
enforceable law over infrastructure they control, but through staged
announcements: export thresholds, safety summits, compute pledges, and grand
infrastructure imaginaries that project resolve where enforceable control is
often absent. We named this condition meta-sovereignty, a mode of rule sustained
by infrastructures that do not yet exist, by fictions that persuade before they
materialize, and by costly signals that stand in for control (Reshef Kera et al.
2026). That paper read the performance through the European Union's 2025 "AI
Continent" plan alongside United States and Chinese initiatives, and it closed by
proposing that sovereignty in this domain is best understood as a dramaturgy,
something continually rehearsed and re-staged rather than possessed.

This article takes that closing proposal seriously as a methodological problem.
If sovereignty is performed, then the analytically decisive layer is not the
finished announcement but the performance in progress: the moment-to-moment work
of keeping a signal credible and a fiction coherent, and the points at which that
work fails. A retrospective reading of pledges cannot reach this layer. By the
time an announcement is available for analysis, the performance has already been
edited into a result. What has been missing is an object that lets us watch the
performance run forward and, crucially, intervene in it.

We build one. Synthēdrion is a human-AI roundtable in which three
language-model agents argue as China, the United States, and the European Union.
Each runs a different underlying model and reads from its own curated corpus of
policy documents, so the three do not merely restate a single machine's priors.
Around them, humans do the work that makes the exercise more than a demo. They
set the opening question, they interrupt and reprompt when an argument settles
too comfortably, and they summon guest agents built to push: an outsider
peace-builder, a crypto-native analyst who trusts no stated motive, and a
critical archivist whose target is the evidence the others lean on. The room can
also switch the register of the performance, staging the same rivalry as a
propaganda studio that emits slogans and posters, or as a mirror-world that
splits every claim into an official line, a buried reality, and a speculation.

We are explicit from the outset about what this apparatus is not. It does not
forecast geopolitical outcomes, it does not measure any state's real capability
or intention, and, because the models are trained on the same public discourse
the roundtable examines, it cannot treat their output as independent evidence
about the world. It studies something narrower and, we argue, more tractable:
the legibility, circulation, and contestation of sovereignty imaginaries. Within
that boundary it does something a text of announcements cannot. It externalizes
the maintenance work of performed sovereignty and makes its fracture points
observable. We call this use of the apparatus a synthetic rehearsal.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reconstructs a genealogy that moves
from conflict-as-text to performed authority, drawing Derrida, Fearon, and
Ezrahi into meta-sovereignty and situating the whole against the recent turn of
compute into statecraft. Section 3 states the analytical gap this genealogy
leaves open and introduces synthetic rehearsal as the concept that fills it.
Section 4 poses our research questions. Section 5 specifies the instrument.
Section 6 reports what running it surfaced, including whose performances the
apparatus recognizes as sovereign and whose it renders as mere terrain. Section 7
returns each inherited concept to the evidence, and Section 8 states the
contribution and its limits.

## 2. From control to performance: a genealogy

*[This section is written as a sequence of conceptual transformations, not a
literature summary. Each concept enters to do one job. Full prose to follow;
the moves are fixed below. Anchor: Eliyahu Keller for 2.1, Denisa/Merav/Hila for
2.4.]*

**2.1 Origin: conflict was already textual.** Begin with Derrida's claim, in "No
Apocalypse, Not Now" (1984), that nuclear war is "fabulously textual," a
phenomenon whose essence is that it depends more than any prior weaponry on
structures of information, communication, and code, and whose ultimate referent
never arrives as an event. The Cold War's decisive object lived in archives,
doctrines, and signals. This is our origin point: great-power conflict was text
and signal before it was ever materiel. Derrida gives us the intuition but not a
mechanism, and his object is a non-event, which is exactly why it cannot by
itself explain how a textual performance produces *credible* authority in real
time. [Eliyahu: also seed the Archive Fever line here, "there is no political
power without control of the archive," which we return to as a power lens and
which our critical-archivist agent literally enacts.]

**2.2 Domain extension and the credibility mechanism: Fearon.** Rationalist
signaling theory (Fearon 1995) supplies what Derrida withholds: an account of how
credibility is manufactured, through commitments made visible and costly enough
that a bluff would not pay. This lets us say why a signal is believed. But
Fearon's signal is a discrete act, sincere or not, sent and read. It is silent on
the case that concerns us, where a performance of resolve must be *sustained*
across many turns and survive repeated challenge.

**2.3 The coherence mechanism: Ezrahi's necessary fictions.** Ezrahi supplies the
second half of the mechanism: legitimacy is held not only by costly signals but
by fictions coherent enough to be inhabited, the imaginative scaffolding that
makes an order feel real. His account was written for the mass-media democracies;
it did not anticipate fictions that are machine-generated and promptable, which
is where our object departs from it.

**2.4 The synthesis and its limit: meta-sovereignty.** Meta-sovereignty (Reshef
Kera et al. 2026) fuses these: authority performed through the co-production of
costly signals and necessary fictions, projecting agency amid structural
interdependence, "a mode of rule sustained by infrastructures that do not yet
exist." This is the concept we inherit. Its limit, for present purposes, is that
it reads the co-production after the fact, from the pledges states have already
staged. It describes the performance; it does not give us the performance as a
running, perturbable object.

**2.5 Contemporary intensification: compute as statecraft.** Situate all of this
against 2025-26, when access to compute began to behave like an instrument of
alliance-building rather than a market good: the sovereign-AI wave counted across
dozens of states yet dependent on foreign chips, clouds, and models (CNAS);
control extending from chip exports to access to frontier models themselves
(CSIS); the reported use of chip access in regional peace diplomacy (WSJ); and
the paradox of Gulf infrastructure investment that deepens dependence even as it
performs autonomy (IISS). The medium of the performance is now the stack itself.

**2.6 Counterpoints, kept in the room.** Two objections must be represented
fairly rather than dissolved. The materialist one holds that AI power reduces to
who holds the hardware: chips, fabs, rare earths, and the physical territory of
data centers (Spitz). The cooperationist one holds that rivalry is not the only
available script, and that concrete collaboration recurs even amid competition
(Slachmuijlder). We do not answer these by denying them. We show, in Section 6,
that in the rehearsal even the materialist claim is *deployed* as a credibility
move, timed to rhetorical need, and that the cooperationist script can be
summoned into the room and observed to hold or fail.

**2.7 Power lens: who is cast as an actor.** Finally, a performance has a cast
and an audience, and casting is power. Ajibona's critique of digital colonialism
reframes the question the three-actor grid suppresses: what does sovereignty mean
for states that enter the story only as territory, market, or mineral? And
Usher's account of the AI Cold War's shadow economy names a backstage, a layer of
money and influence that resists the archive entirely. Both tell us that the
performance is not equally legible for everyone, and that some of it is staged
where the record cannot follow. We take these as the power lens on our own
instrument: it will recognize as sovereign whatever it is built and prompted to
recognize, and we have to watch that casting rather than trust it.

## 3. Bridge: from description to apparatus

Put the genealogy together and a specific, bounded gap appears. Meta-sovereignty
tells us that authority in the AI Cold War is performed through signals and
fictions rather than held through control, but it presupposes a retrospective
vantage, the finished pledge. Fearon names why a signal is credible but treats it
as a single act. Ezrahi names why a fiction coheres but not how a coherence is
defended when someone in the room refuses it. What none of them provides, and
what the object itself demands, is an account of the performance as it is
maintained, repaired, and broken under live contestation.

The reason this gap has stayed open is practical, not only conceptual. The
maintenance work of sovereignty is ordinarily invisible because it is only ever
encountered as its product. A summit communiqué, an export rule, an "AI
Continent" plan: each arrives already edited into a result, with the labor that
produced it, the near-failures, the concessions, the moments a claim almost did
not hold, removed. To study the labor you would need to run the performance
forward, under conditions you could vary, with the freedom to push on it and see
what gives. That is what a synthetic roundtable provides.

We define **synthetic rehearsal** as the use of a multi-agent language-model
staging, with human intervention and prompt design as controls, to externalize
and observe the maintenance work of performed sovereignty. The term is chosen
against its neighbors. It is not a simulation in the predictive sense; nothing
here forecasts. It is not theatre in the illustrative sense; the point is not to
dramatize a thesis but to make a mechanism observable. Rehearsal is exact: a
rehearsal is a performance run for the sake of watching how it is put together,
where it can be stopped, restarted, and pushed, and where a director's
intervention is a legitimate part of the method rather than a contamination of
it. The humans who interrupt and summon are not noise in the experiment. They are
the instrument's active variable.

Three commitments follow from the definition and discipline everything after it.
First, the apparatus yields output-level evidence only, transcripts, generated
artifacts, and recorded shifts, and we make no claim about the models' internal
representations or about any real state's intentions. Second, authority is never
located in a model. It is distributed across the arrangement: the models, the
per-actor corpora, the prompts, the interface, the humans in the room, the
audience, and whatever later circulates. When an agent's claim reads as sovereign
it is because the arrangement, including its human recognizers, made it so.
Third, the instrument carries a reflexivity limit that we build in rather than
apologize for. The models are trained on the same discourse we study, so the
rehearsal can reproduce the imaginaries it is meant to interrogate. Eliyahu's
inversion of the old acronym names the danger precisely: Mutually Assured
Destruction becomes Model Autophagy Disorder, the collapse that comes when a
system is fed its own output until nothing new remains. The rehearsal studies the
circulation of sovereignty imaginaries; it must not be mistaken for an
independent measurement of the world those imaginaries describe.

What we gain for the price of these limits is the missing object. With the
performance running forward and open to intervention, maintenance-under-
contestation becomes observable in a way it never is in the finished pledge. When
a human summons the archivist and a consensus that looked settled comes apart,
we are watching a fiction fail to cohere under pressure. When the same prompt run
twice produces a bloc that hardens once and concedes once, we are watching the
signal being re-manufactured rather than simply sent. These are the events
Section 6 reports. Section 5 specifies the instrument that produces them.

## 5. Method: the roundtable as instrument

*[Writing brief. Anchor: Avi Spitz for the infrastructure/territory grounding of
the corpora; technical apparatus described from the running system.]*

- The agents: three state actors, distinct models, distinct per-actor policy
  corpora (name them and cite the corpus documents, including the collaborators'
  own papers used as source material). State plainly that the distinct-model,
  distinct-corpus design is what lets us test whether difference is real or
  merely staged (ties to the falsification indicator).
- The three registers as three views on the same performance: debate,
  propaganda-lab (slogan + generated poster per turn), mirror-world (official
  line / buried reality / speculation, closing on a front page). Explain that the
  registers vary how the performance is made legible, which is itself data.
- The controls: the opening prompt, human interruption and re-prompting, and
  guest-agent summons (peace-builder, analyst, archivist). Describe each as a
  perturbation with an expected effect, per the ledger.
- Measurement discipline: what we record (turns, speaker, prompt in force, guest
  summoned, position change and its antecedent turn) and what we refuse to score
  (a numeric "sovereignty" value). Reference the theory-to-evidence ledger.
- The reflexivity control: the "three models trained on the same internet" prompt
  as a direct probe of the autophagy boundary. Report it as a test, not a flourish.
- Ethics / status of artifacts: posters and front pages are generated fictions
  and are labeled as such; no output is presented as a real document.

## 6. What the rehearsal surfaced

*[Writing brief. Report patterns without letting a theoretical label do
statistical work. Anchors: Ajibona (power lens), Usher (backstage), Slachmuijlder
(cooperation counter). Draw on real sessions; verify each claim against the
transcripts before it goes in.]*

- **Recurrent tropes across runs.** Exportability of a governance model;
  sovereignty repeatedly relocated into mechanisms (registries, sovereign clouds,
  audits, quantum firewalls); ideals colliding with dependencies in every bloc.
  Report as recurrence, with the caveat that recurrence across model-distinct
  agents is the interesting part.
- **Novel options, not just restatement.** The drone-warfare session's EU
  "public compute auction house" proposal as an option surfaced in play; check it
  against the loaded corpus to distinguish generation from retrieval.
- **Fracture under contestation.** Cases where summoning a counter-agent broke a
  consensus; contrast with runs left un-summoned. This is RQ1's core evidence.
- **The materialist claim as a performed move.** China's rare-earth-and-GPS
  "small players stay tethered" argument and the United States "ecosystem
  chokepoint" claim, read as credibility moves timed to rhetorical need, not as
  neutral facts entering the room. This is how we answer the materialist
  counterpoint without denying it.
- **Casting and the backstage.** Where Global South actors appear only as
  territory/market/mineral unless explicitly written in (Ajibona), and where the
  debate gestures at an off-book layer it cannot represent (Usher). Present as the
  power-lens finding and as a limit on the instrument's recognition.
- **The cooperation script.** What the peace-builder agent surfaced and whether
  it held under pressure (Slachmuijlder).
- **The autophagy probe.** What the reflexive prompt produced, reported honestly
  as evidence for or against genuine inter-model difference.

## 7. Discussion

*[Return to each inherited concept and state continuity, revision, or boundary.]*

- Derrida: continuity confirmed and made operable; the textual layer is now not
  only readable but runnable.
- Fearon: revised; the signal is not a discrete act but a maintained
  performance, and the rehearsal shows the maintenance.
- Ezrahi: extended into machine-generated, promptable fictions, with the
  autophagy caveat.
- Meta-sovereignty: sharpened from a description of what states do into an
  account of the labor by which they keep doing it, and equipped with an
  instrument.
- The power lens (Ajibona, Usher): stated as a standing limit on the instrument,
  not solved.

## 8. Conclusion

*[State both contribution and non-claim.]* The contribution is synthetic
rehearsal: a method that makes performed sovereignty observable and
stress-testable, and a corresponding sharpening of meta-sovereignty. The
non-claim is equally important: the rehearsal forecasts nothing, measures no
capability, and, bounded by autophagy, studies the circulation of imaginaries
rather than the world they invoke. Open the research programme, name the tests in
the ledger that remain to be run, and do not convert any hypothesis into a fact.
