# EU AI Governance Country Profile (Alan Turing Institute, 2025)

> Sources: Leone de Castris, The Alan Turing Institute, "AI Governance around the world: European Union", 2025-08
> Raw: [AI Governance around the world — EU](../../raw/newpapers/ai-governance-around-the-world-eu.md)
> Updated: 2026-08-13

## The hard-law posture

- The EU takes a harder AI governance approach than most jurisdictions: legally binding requirements to minimise risk to health, safety, and fundamental rights, with extraterritorial reach — the rules apply to products commercialised in the EU market and to those affecting persons residing in the EU even if never introduced there.
- The EU AI Act (in force 1 Aug 2024) regulates by risk tier keyed to intended use: unacceptable-risk applications are prohibited outright, high-risk systems carry stringent requirements, and the EU AI Office (created Feb 2024) is the primary implementation body.
- GPAI model providers carry their own obligations (technical documentation, enabling downstream compliance); providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must additionally run standardised model evaluations, assess and mitigate Union-level systemic risks, report serious incidents to the AI Office, and secure their models and infrastructure.

## Standards as the load-bearing wall

- European harmonised standards, developed by the European Standards Organisations on Commission request, provide the technical specifications implementing the AI Act: formally voluntary, but adoption grants a presumption of conformity — making standards bodies the quiet arbiters of what compliance means.

## From regulation to competitiveness

- The centre of gravity is shifting from rulemaking to industrial strategy: the AI Continent Action Plan (Apr 2025) aims to position the EU as a global AI leader, and the GPAI Code of Practice (Aug 2025) is the voluntary bridge helping providers comply with Articles 53 and 55.
- Coming initiatives stack competitiveness on top of compliance: the Apply AI Strategy (targeting AI uptake in key sectors and public services), the European Strategy for AI in Science, and a Cloud and AI Development Act expected by Q1 2026 to incentivise European compute build-out.
- The strategic through-line: Brussels bets that binding rules plus harmonised standards give it standard-setting power beyond its borders, while the newer industrial plans concede that regulatory gravity without domestic compute and adoption is leverage on someone else's infrastructure.
