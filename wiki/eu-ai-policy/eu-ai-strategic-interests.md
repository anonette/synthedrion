## Overview

The EU-specific corpus is still lighter than the China and U.S. folders, but it now has a sharper strategic profile. The new Financial Times source shows the EU moving from a largely regulatory posture toward a more explicit promotion of European cloud, data-center, semiconductor, and AI alternatives. An EU agent should therefore read AI not only through standards and governance legitimacy, but through infrastructure sovereignty and industrial capacity.

The newer Gulf-capital material already sharpened a structural weakness in that profile: European sovereignty ambitions are also a finance and energy problem. The FT article makes that weakness politically explicit by showing Brussels trying to accelerate data-center construction, define levels of cloud sovereignty, and reduce dependence on U.S. hyperscalers that currently dominate more than 70 percent of the EU cloud market.

The most important shift is conceptual. Europe is no longer only asking how to regulate foreign AI power. It is more directly asking how to build enough domestic infrastructure that regulation does not remain a sovereignty gesture without technical backing.

## Provisional Interests

- shape trustworthy governance and evaluation standards
- preserve regulatory and digital sovereignty
- strengthen European cloud and compute capacity so governance ambitions are not hollow
- avoid dependence on U.S.-China rivalry logics alone
- support multilateral and institution-building approaches
- improve the financing and energy terms of European compute expansion so sovereignty is not only legal or rhetorical
- use cloud and AI development policy to create European alternatives rather than only disciplining U.S. incumbents
