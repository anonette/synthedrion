## Overview

The new infrastructure corpus changes the project's baseline. AI sovereignty can no longer be read mainly as data control, model ownership, or legal jurisdiction. It now appears as operational control over a tightly coupled stack: data centers, power, cooling, water, optical networks, telemetry, and the mineral supply chains that make hardware possible.

## Sovereignty Is an Infrastructure Condition

`AI Infrastructure Sovereignty` argues that control over AI systems increasingly depends on whether an actor can actually build, power, cool, connect, observe, and adapt infrastructure under physical constraints. The key distinction is between legal sovereignty and operational sovereignty. Legal authority matters, but it does not by itself guarantee that an actor can sustain frontier training, resilient inference, or independent deployment when electricity, water, latency, and network reach become binding constraints.

This is useful for the wiki because it sharpens an existing intuition already present in the compute and Gulf-capital pages: AI competition is not only about chips or models. It is about whether actors can hold together a feasible operating region across compute, energy, and logistics.

## Energy, Water, and Fiber Are Strategic Chokepoints

The infrastructure paper and the JPMorgan geopolitics report converge on the same point from different angles. Training clusters and large inference fleets require megawatt-scale power, high-density cooling, and low-latency optical interconnects. That turns electricity headroom, water availability, carbon intensity, and network topology into strategic variables rather than background operational details.

JPMorgan's report generalizes this into a geopolitical claim: energy and hardware are now core AI chokepoints, while the infrastructure paper makes the physical mechanism explicit. The result is a stronger shared frame for the whole knowledge base: actors that cannot secure energy and high-capacity infrastructure may remain formally sovereign while becoming practically dependent.

## Community Resistance Is Part of the Infrastructure Story

The Axios datacenter article adds a domestic-political layer missing from the more strategic papers. It shows that datacenter buildout also creates local backlash around electricity bills, water use, noise, and land-use change. Even though the article centers on alleged foreign amplification, its more important contribution for this wiki is that AI infrastructure is becoming politically legible at the community level.

This matters because sovereignty is not only an interstate question. It is also mediated by zoning fights, permitting, public tolerance, and the ability to legitimize large infrastructure projects inside democratic and quasi-democratic systems.

## Critical Minerals Extend the Constraint Chain

The FP Analytics minerals brief extends the infrastructure story upstream. AI depends on minerals such as copper, gallium, germanium, palladium, tantalum, rare earth elements, silicon, and high-purity alumina. That means infrastructure sovereignty begins before the data center itself, at extraction, refining, and trade concentration. The brief emphasizes Chinese dominance in several refining chokepoints and shows that AI demand compounds clean-energy demand rather than standing apart from it.

Taken together, these sources make a stronger claim than the prior wiki: compute sovereignty is a chain of dependencies, not a single policy decision. It runs from mines and refining to grids, cables, water permits, and telemetry.

## Relevance for Actor Knowledge Bases

For a U.S. agent, this page adds a reason to treat energy buildout, grid reform, and hardware supply security as part of AI strategy rather than adjacent industrial policy. For a China agent, it reinforces the logic of state-led coordination across energy, infrastructure, and manufacturing. For an EU agent, it clarifies why sovereignty claims fail if they are not backed by data-center capacity, financing, and resilient supply chains.
