# AI, Nuclear Posture, and Human Control

> Sources: SIPRI, 2026-02
> Raw: `../../raw/nuclear/2602_ai_integration.pdf`
> Updated: 2026-06-07

## Overview

This source adds a sharper view of the AI-nuclear nexus than the current wiki had before. Its main claim is comparative: China, India, and the United States are all integrating AI-related functions into nuclear or nuclear-related systems, but they differ greatly in transparency, doctrine, and how clearly they define human control.

## Human Control Exists, But Unevenly and Ambiguously

The SIPRI backgrounder shows that the United States is the most explicit in official doctrine, insisting that humans remain in the loop for nuclear-weapon employment decisions. China has publicly endorsed human control over relevant weapon systems and, together with the United States, affirmed the importance of human control over decisions to use nuclear weapons, but remains more opaque about where those limits apply in practice. India stresses human judgment and oversight in autonomous weapons debates while saying little publicly about AI in its own nuclear posture.

The key implication is that agreement on principle does not yet amount to a stable shared operational norm.

## Dual-Use Systems Blur the Boundary

The report is especially useful because it does not reduce the issue to launch authority alone. It surveys AI-related functions in early warning, decision support, reconnaissance, simulation, autonomy, predictive maintenance, and dual-capable platforms. That widens the risk surface. Strategic instability can emerge not only from an autonomous launch decision, but from AI entering the wider systems that shape warning, targeting, classification, and escalation pressure.

This fits well with the existing rivalry material in the repo. The danger is not just a single rogue system. It is a growing mesh of AI-enabled military functions whose nuclear and conventional roles may be hard to disentangle during crisis.

## Opacity Itself Is a Strategic Problem

The SIPRI paper repeatedly returns to asymmetry in public information. The United States offers more official detail, China less, and India least of all. That means confidence-building is made harder not only by technical change, but by uneven doctrinal visibility. In effect, uncertainty about what each side means by human control, meaningful control, or human judgment becomes part of the risk environment.

## Relevance for Actor Knowledge Bases

For a U.S. agent, this page supports arguments for preserving human control and limiting instability even while integrating AI into wider defense systems. For a China agent, it highlights the tradeoff between strategic ambiguity and the confidence costs of opacity. For the broader shared layer, it adds a concrete reason to treat AI governance, military modernization, and strategic stability as tightly linked rather than separable topics.
