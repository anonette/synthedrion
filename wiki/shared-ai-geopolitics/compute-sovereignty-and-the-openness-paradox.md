## Overview

"Sovereignty" is the most overloaded word in AI geopolitics. This page separates its distinct meanings so agents can use them precisely, and explains why open-source AI — which looks like a surrender of control — is in practice a sovereignty instrument. It also frames why the three blocs' governance models are not different implementations of shared values but genuinely incompatible logics.

## Three Levels of Compute Sovereignty

Compute sovereignty decomposes into three measurable levels: how much AI compute sits on a country's territory; the nationality of the firms owning the data centers; and the nationality of the accelerator vendors supplying the chips. A country can hold one level without the others — territorial compute on foreign-owned hardware running foreign chips is not the same as full-stack sovereignty. Because six hyperscalers (AWS, Google, Microsoft, Alibaba, Tencent, Huawei) dominate the market, most countries must trade off across levels and either hedge or align between U.S.- and Chinese-owned compute. Post-Snowden, the U.S. CLOUD Act and FISA reach make U.S.-owned providers a sovereignty risk for others, which drives "trusted vendor" policies.

## Cyber, Data, and AI Sovereignty in Chinese Practice

China's cyber sovereignty doctrine treats the state as the supreme rule-maker over its cyberspace and rejects the Western multistakeholder model. It is an extension of a state-centric view of international order, driven partly by fear of sovereignty erosion through external information flows. Data sovereignty is operationalized through the Data Security Law, PIPL, and the 2023 Interim Measures for Generative AI Services, which force local deployment and content alignment with "Socialist Core Values." AI sovereignty is the capability to train and run frontier models domestically, pursued through the National Integrated Computing Power Network and "Eastern Data, Western Computing," and the "whole-of-nation system" meant to overcome the over-dispersion of the "battle of a hundred models."

## The Openness Paradox

China's embrace of open-source AI only appears to contradict cyber sovereignty. Open weights are repurposed to reinforce control at every layer: they route around hardware sanctions ("software supplements hardware," with DeepSeek reportedly training on a fraction of comparable Western compute), substitute the U.S. software stack with domestic frameworks, keep data in-country through local deployment, embed content filtering, and project soft power across the Global South under an "AI democratization" narrative that creates standards path-dependency. Openness here serves cyber-sovereign ends, not liberal ones.

## Three Incompatible Governance Logics

The EU runs a horizontal, risk-tiered AI Act expected to project a "Brussels Effect." China runs a hybrid: top-down on safety and social harmony, bottom-up on provincial innovation, expressed through sector-specific laws and a CAC filing regime with extraterritorial reach. The United States is fragmented and deregulatory, wielding compute export controls as its primary lever. These encode different ultimate values — dignity and human oversight, social stability and state security, capability and competitiveness — so AI agents built under each carry conflicting objectives. The incompatibility "arises not from the absence of governance but from the presence of fundamentally incompatible governance philosophies."

## Relevance for Actor Knowledge Bases

For a China agent, sovereignty language is both shield and sword: it legitimizes domestic control and frames open-model diffusion as generosity rather than influence. For a U.S. agent, the leverage is the compute chokepoint and the discipline of standards, but its own providers' extraterritorial reach is what makes others want to escape it. For an EU agent, the play is to be the rule-maker and trusted-vendor anchor even without frontier models — and to exploit the contradiction between others' openness rhetoric and their control practices.
