# Energy, the Power Wall, and Data-Center Build-Out

## Overview

The binding constraint on AI is shifting from chips to electricity, grid interconnection, and cooling water. This page treats power as a geopolitical variable: who can build generation and transmission fast enough sets the ceiling on compute, and the externalities of that build-out become a legitimacy battleground.

## The Demand Curve

Data-center electricity demand is projected to roughly double from about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to around 950 terawatt-hours by 2030, reaching roughly 3% of global electricity; AI-focused consumption triples over the period. In 2025 alone, global data-center electricity grew 17% and AI-focused demand surged about 50% in a single year. The 2035 spread across scenarios runs from 700 to 1,700 terawatt-hours. The United States accounts for about 45% of data-center electricity, China about 25%, and Europe about 15% — together roughly 85%.

## The Power Wall: Grids, Not Chips

Grid interconnection queues are now the sharpest bottleneck. In Texas, the ERCOT large-load queue (mostly data centers) exploded from 63 gigawatts in late 2024 to over 230 gigawatts by early 2026, against an all-time state peak demand of only about 85 gigawatts. Alberta's connection requests exceeded provincial peak demand, forcing a regulator-imposed 1.2-gigawatt cap. New transmission takes four to eight years, transformer and cable lead times have doubled, and an AI data center is roughly ten times more capital-intensive than an aluminum smelter, so curtailing it to relieve the grid is very costly. Clustering compounds the strain: data centers are about 1.5% of global electricity but 20 to 30% of demand in the metros where they cluster.

## How Each Pole Powers the Build-Out

The United States is the onsite-gas pioneer: over 100 gigawatts of new gas is planned as dedicated data-center supply, more than a third of all new global gas capacity. China leans on coal baseload (around 20% of its data-center supply) while routing load west under "Eastern Data, Western Computing" toward wind- and solar-rich inland provinces. The Gulf adds roughly 10 gigawatts. Nuclear is re-entering through small modular reactors, whose offtake pipeline grew from 25 to 45 gigawatts in a year, with first units around 2030. Renewables plus gas are projected to supply over 65% of data-center electricity by 2030.

## The Externality Ledger

By 2030 data centers may consume over 945 terawatt-hours, emit close to 400 million tons of CO2-equivalent, withdraw on the order of 9 trillion liters of water (comparable to annual basic water for all of Sub-Saharan Africa), and occupy over 14,500 square kilometers of land. AI infrastructure could generate up to 2.5 million metric tons of e-waste per year by 2030. Inference, not training, drives 80 to 90% of AI energy. These costs concentrate in specific communities — arid siting regions, mining zones, and the Global South — which turns the energy story into a legitimacy story.

## Relevance for Actor Knowledge Bases

For a U.S. agent, energy build-out speed is a genuine advantage but carries grid-strain and social-license backlash. For a China agent, coal plus western routing buys scale at an emissions-legitimacy cost it must manage rhetorically. For an EU agent, slower grids are a real bottleneck, but its standards leadership and industrial-automation strength offer a different lever. For all actors, water and emissions externalities are a shared vulnerability that rivals can weaponize as hypocrisy.
