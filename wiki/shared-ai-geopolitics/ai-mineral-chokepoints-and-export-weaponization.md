# AI Mineral Chokepoints and Export Weaponization

## Overview

This page makes the mineral base of AI precise enough to argue about. The decisive leverage in the AI hardware supply chain is not who holds reserves but who controls **midstream refining and processing** — the stage where China is dominant and where rivals are most exposed. It also tracks how mineral exports have become an instrument of coercion between 2023 and 2025, turning supply chains into a domain of weaponized interdependence.

## The Chokepoint Map: Mining Versus Refining

The recurring structural insight is that China's leverage sits midstream, because it accepts environmental externalities that others will not.

- Gallium: China supplied roughly 98% of unprocessed and 86% of total gallium in 2022; it is a byproduct of bauxite-to-aluminum processing, where China holds about 60% of capacity. CSIS calls gallium the material with the highest disruption potential of any semiconductor input.
- Germanium: China controls about 65% of supply; the market is tiny (around 140 metric tons of primary output), so it is easy to throttle.
- Rare earth elements (neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium): China holds roughly 60% of mining and about 90% of processing and refining; magnets are essential to data-center motors and hard drives.
- Cobalt: the DRC is the largest producer, but China refines about 90% of global cobalt.
- Copper: faces a potential 30% supply shortfall by 2035 from declining ore grades and long lead times; a single large data center needs over 1,000 tons of copper, and data centers add about 1 million metric tons of copper demand by 2030.
- Graphite, lithium, nickel: China dominates anode graphite and battery-grade processing (75% of LFP cathode material, 95% of high-purity manganese sulphate); refining concentration is intensifying, not easing.

The danger is bundling: cutting gallium, germanium, and arsenic together would strip chipmakers of multiple high-performance substitutes at once.

## Export Controls as Graduated Coercion

Mineral exports have shifted from background risk to active statecraft. In July and August 2023 China restricted gallium and germanium in response to U.S. chip controls, and prices rose sharply (gallium more than doubled). In December 2024 it restricted antimony and tightened gallium and germanium specifically toward the United States. In April 2025 it added seven heavy rare earths plus tungsten, tellurium, bismuth, indium, and molybdenum. More than half of energy-related minerals are now under some export control. A licensing regime lets Beijing flip from a presumption of approval to a presumption of denial without a formal ban.

## Coercion Can Backfire

The 2010 Chinese rare-earth embargo on Japan triggered a $1.2 billion Japanese mitigation fund and a decade of diversification; the neon shortage after 2022 (40 to 50% had come from Russia and Ukraine) recovered within a year. Export coercion buys short-term leverage but accelerates the diversification it is meant to prevent. Meanwhile the U.S. National Defense Stockpile has shrunk from about $9.6 billion in 1989 to under $1 billion today, and gallium is not even on the list, so the buffer against coercion is thin.

## Relevance for Actor Knowledge Bases

For a China agent, midstream dominance is the crown jewel of leverage, but it is wasting leverage that diversification erodes if used too openly. For a U.S. agent, the exposure is concrete: fourteen critical minerals have zero domestic production, the stockpile is depleted, and chip leadership rests on inputs Beijing controls. For an EU agent, this confirms that sovereignty is upstream of cloud and data centers; access to politically reliable processing is the binding constraint.
