# Stoler — Colonial Archives and the Arts of Governance (2002)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- The methodological move: treat archives not as sites of knowledge retrieval but as sites of knowledge production, reading "along the archival grain" to study how the archive itself was made before reading against it for hidden voices.
- Colonial archives were technologies of rule in themselves: "the archive was the supreme technology of the late nineteenth-century imperial state," monuments of states and instruments of governance, not neutral repositories about them.
- Archives should be studied as "cross-sections of contested knowledge": what was written, copied, forwarded, filed, or omitted traces the anxieties, taxonomies, and epistemic habits of the administration that produced them.
- Archival conventions — categories, forms of address, what warranted a report — encode the state's "epistemological uncertainties" as much as its confidence; the gaps, redundancies, and mistakes are data about how power thought.
- Ethnography of the archive: the anthropologist's fieldsite extends to the filing system; provenance, circulation, and classification of documents are social facts with politics of their own.
- Colonial statecraft ran on paper: the production of documents, their duplication and routing, disciplined both the governed and the governors, defining what officials could perceive as an event, a threat, or a population.
- What the archive renders unimaginable matters as much as what it records: certain persons and happenings could not appear in the record at all, or only as categories (native, unrest, sentiment) that pre-formatted their political meaning.
