# Ketelaar — Archival Temples, Archival Prisons: Modes of Power and Protection (2002)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- The double metaphor: archives function simultaneously as temples (sites of trust, authenticity, and veneration that legitimate power) and prisons (instruments of surveillance and control that hold people captive in their records).
- Records used to discipline: registration, identification papers, dossiers, and files subject individuals to what Ketelaar, following Foucault, treats as panoptical power — one can be known, sorted, and acted upon through one's records.
- The same records that imprison can protect: registration that enabled persecution also enables restitution, pension claims, and proof of rights; the archive's political valence depends on who controls access and use, not on the records alone.
- The temple aspect is performative: the solemnity of archival buildings, reading-room rituals, and certification practices manufacture the trustworthiness that gives records their social power as evidence.
- Archivalization (Ketelaar's coinage): before anything is archived, social and cultural factors determine what is even considered worth recording — a filter operating prior to and invisible within every archive.
- Every activation of a record — each reading, citation, or migration — adds meaning; records are not fixed but continuously re-semanticized by use, so the archive is a process, not a place.
- The archivist's ethical task is to keep the prison honest and the temple open: transparency about surveillance uses, and access regimes that let the governed use the state's records against the state.
