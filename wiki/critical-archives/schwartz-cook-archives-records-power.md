# Schwartz & Cook — Archives, Records, and Power: The Making of Modern Memory (2002)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- "Archives are not passive storehouses of old stuff, but active sites where social power is negotiated, contested, confirmed"; through archives the past is controlled, and certain stories are privileged while others are marginalized.
- Archives wield "power over the shape and direction of historical scholarship, collective memory, and national identity, over how we know ourselves as individuals, groups, and societies" — yet in the archival literature this power is rarely acknowledged.
- The central insight: records are not neutral by-products of activity but constructed instruments; every record was created by someone, for some purpose, within some relation of power, and every archival intervention (appraisal, description, arrangement) reshapes it again.
- Archivists' professional myth of neutrality and objectivity is itself the source of their power: the more invisible and "merely technical" archival work appears, the less accountable its shaping force becomes.
- Appraisal — deciding what is kept and what is destroyed — is the archivist's most political act: the archivist determines what the future will know of the past, creating "the archive" that later generations mistake for the past itself.
- Classification schemes, descriptive standards, and finding aids are technologies of power: naming, ordering, and describing records determines their retrievability and therefore their capacity to support or subvert narratives.
- Control of memory institutions is a strategic asset for states and dominant groups; struggles over archives are struggles over what a society can legitimately claim happened, and thus over present authority.
- The remedy is not perfect neutrality but disclosure: archivists should make their interventions visible and accountable rather than pretending the archive assembled itself.
