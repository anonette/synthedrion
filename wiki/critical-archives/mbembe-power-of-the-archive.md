# Mbembe — The Power of the Archive and its Limits (2002)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- The archive is first a building and a collection of documents belonging to the state: its very architecture — the temple-like structure, the rituals of access — confers an imagined status on the fragments kept inside, so the archive is "not a piece of data, but a status."
- Archives are the product of a double process of judgment: someone decided certain documents deserved preservation and others deserved discard; the archive is therefore founded on acts of discrimination it later conceals.
- No archive can be the depository of an entire society's history; what it holds are fragments, "montages of fragments," which create the illusion of totality and continuity where there is none.
- The archive is a form of sepulchre: documents of the dead are interred, and the state, through the archive, both honors the dead and imprisons them, deciding which fragments of past lives remain speakable.
- The state's power over the archive includes the power of destruction: chronophagy, the state "eating time" by destroying or prohibiting archives, is the twin of commemoration; archiving and destroying are both techniques of rule.
- Against the state's use, the examined archive can also be a talisman against the state's own lies: the fragment resists total rewriting, which is exactly why regimes fear, seal, or burn records.
- The historian's relation to the archive is a ritual of resurrection with rules: material can only enter discourse through the archive's authorized forms, so what counts as evidence is decided before any argument begins.
