# Caswell, Punzalan & Sangwand — Critical Archival Studies: An Introduction (2017)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- Critical archival studies is defined as approaches that (1) explain what is unjust with the current state of archival research and practice, (2) posit practical goals for how it can change, and (3) provide the norms by which to judge that transformation — theory for and about archives with an emancipatory aim.
- The field takes from critical theory the premise that scholarship is never neutral: archival research and practice are interventions in power relations, whether acknowledged or not.
- Archives are implicated in the production of symbolic annihilation: communities absent from, misrepresented in, or maligned by dominant archives are harmed in their capacity to assert identity, rights, and history.
- Key research strands: community archives as counter-institutions, radical empathy and feminist ethics in appraisal and description, decolonizing provenance, records as instruments of both oppression and accountability.
- Human rights framing: records function as evidence for redress and transitional justice, yet the same bureaucratic records that document atrocity were instruments of committing it — the archive's dual capacity.
- Appraisal, description, and access are ethical decisions, not technical ones: metadata fields, naming conventions, and finding aids encode whose worldview organizes the record.
- The call to action: archivists and scholars should move from describing archival power to redistributing it — participatory description, post-custodial models, community control over records about communities.
