# Zinn — Secrecy, Archives, and the Public Interest (1970/1977)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- Zinn's charge to the profession: the archivist's claimed neutrality is a political stance in disguise; "the archivist, even more than the historian and the political scientist, tends to be scrupulous about his neutrality, and to see his job as a technical job... but I will stick to my claim... professionalism is a powerful form of social control."
- Archives reproduce the inequalities of the society that creates them: the rich and powerful leave abundant, well-organized records; the poor leave fragments, and mostly appear in the records others kept about them (police, welfare, employers).
- Government secrecy inverts the proper order: the state collects ever more information about citizens while citizens have decreasing access to information about the state; surveillance grows as transparency shrinks.
- The bias of the archive is compounded at every stage: what is recorded, what is collected, what is preserved, what is opened, and what researchers are encouraged to use each tilt toward the powerful.
- Zinn's proposals: archivists should actively document the lives of ordinary people, collect the records of protest movements and the powerless, and campaign to open the state's closed records rather than waiting politely.
- Archival "activism" is not a corruption of the profession but its rescue: refusing to choose is choosing the status quo, because the existing flow of records already has a direction.
- The essay's lasting formula: rather than being passive custodians of whatever descends to them, archivists should compensate for the archive's built-in class bias — a documentation strategy as deliberate counter-power.
