# Carbajal & Caswell — Critical Digital Archives: A Review from Archival Studies (2021)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- The article maps seven themes of digital records scholarship — materiality, appraisal, context, use, scale, relationships, sustainability — arguing that anyone who uses or builds digital collections is doing archival work with archival politics.
- Digital records are material: files depend on formats, storage infrastructures, and migrations; every conversion and platform choice alters what survives and how it can be read.
- Appraisal does not disappear at scale — it hides: web archives, scraped corpora, and mass-digitized collections feel total but are shaped by crawl schedules, robots.txt, copyright, funding, and platform APIs; the selection is automated and therefore less visible.
- Context is the first casualty of digitization: records separated from their provenance, arrangement, and custodial history circulate as free-floating "content," inviting misreading and misuse.
- Scale changes the politics of use: computational reading (search, text mining, machine learning) makes the retrieval algorithm the new finding aid, and ranking functions inherit the appraisal power archivists once held.
- The blurred line between user and archivist: historians and technologists now create the collections they study, so archival ethics — consent, description, community control — apply to dataset builders, not just repositories.
- Sustainability is political: digital archives die without ongoing labor and money, so which collections stay online is a continuous funding decision disguised as a technical fact.
- Community and post-custodial models point forward: records can remain with their creators while being preserved, redistributing archival authority away from central institutions.
