# Bastian — Reading Colonial Records Through an Archival Lens (2006)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- Provenance — the bedrock archival principle that records are organized by their creator — is a political decision: in colonial archives it credits the colonizer as sole creator and erases the colonized, who are in the records but never of them.
- Bastian's expansion: provenance should include the "provenance of place" — the entire community within which records were created, so that a colonized population can be recognized as co-creator of the records that governed it.
- Case in point, the U.S. Virgin Islands: the islands' historical records were removed to Denmark and the United States as the property of the administering states, leaving the community without custody of its own documentary past.
- Custody determines historical voice: a community whose records are held elsewhere must reconstruct its history through the categories, languages, and access regimes of its former rulers.
- Colonial record-keeping systems classified people as objects of administration — property, labor, converts, subjects — and those categories persist as the finding aids through which descendants must search for ancestors.
- Reading "through an archival lens" means asking of any document set: who created it, who kept it, who was allowed to see it, how it was arranged — before asking what it says.
- The redress is archival, not only historiographical: repatriation, shared custody, and re-description that names the whole society of the record are acts of restoring a community's evidence of itself.
