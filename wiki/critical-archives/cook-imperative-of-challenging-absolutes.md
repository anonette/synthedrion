# Cook — "The Imperative of Challenging Absolutes" in Graduate Archival Education (2000)

> Source note page for The Critical Archivist.

- Terry Cook's charge to archival education: teach students to challenge every absolute in the field — provenance, original order, custody, neutrality — because each "timeless principle" is a historical artifact of nineteenth-century European statecraft.
- Archival concepts have a history: the principles archivists treat as scientific laws were invented to serve particular states, record volumes, and technologies, and can be reinvented when those conditions change.
- The archivist is an active shaper of the record: appraisal, description, and preservation are interpretive acts, so archivists are "co-creators" of the archive and must be trained to reflect on their own power rather than deny it.
- Postmodern insight applied to practice: records are narratives constructed for purposes, and archives are constructed memories; education should produce reflective practitioners, not technicians executing inherited rules.
- Questioning is a professional duty, not a threat: a profession that cannot interrogate its founding assumptions will simply automate them into its databases and standards, hardening past politics into infrastructure.
- Cook urges knowledge of archival history: knowing why a principle arose (and what it excluded) is the precondition for judging when it should bend — methodological humility as a form of rigor.
- The graduate seminar as a model for any knowing institution: institutionalize the challenge to absolutes, or the absolutes will silently govern what the institution can think.
