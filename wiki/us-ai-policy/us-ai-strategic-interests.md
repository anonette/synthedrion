## Overview

The current U.S. material in the repository frames AI as a problem of national advantage, resilience, strategic stability, and controlled ecosystem influence. The U.S. is not only trying to lead in model capability. It is trying to preserve favorable international conditions, retain compute and standards leverage, and avoid catastrophic instability during intense competition with China.

The newer Gulf-capital material adds a financial and infrastructural layer: U.S. AI power also depends on whether it can keep large pools of Gulf capital, energy alignment, and hyperscale buildout politically compatible with U.S.-centered compute ecosystems.

The newer infrastructure and geopolitics sources sharpen that further. U.S. advantage now appears tied not only to models and cloud governance, but also to power buildout, datacenter siting, semiconductor and mineral supply security, and the ability to keep AI integration inside defense and nuclear-related systems under visibly human control.

## Mineral Exposure and the Hemispheric Answer

The 2026 mineral corpus exposes the soft underbelly of U.S. advantage. Fourteen critical minerals have zero domestic U.S. production, the National Defense Stockpile has fallen from about $9.6 billion in 1989 to under $1 billion, and gallium — the input with the highest disruption potential — is not even stockpiled. U.S. chip leadership rests on inputs China refines. The response is a build-out of leverage on the manufactured side of the chain (NVIDIA, TSMC access, hyperscaler cloud) paired with friend-shoring: the Minerals Security Partnership, IRA and CHIPS incentives, and a hemispheric push (the proposed Americas Act, Western-Hemisphere copper, lithium, and graphite) against entrenched Chinese positions — China invested about $214 billion in Latin America from 2005 to 2023.

In simulation, a U.S. agent should treat mineral and refining dependence as its most credible vulnerability, and offer partners capital, security, and market access while its own permitting paralysis undercuts the pitch.

## Energy Build-Out and the Talent Base

Power is now a binding constraint and a U.S. advantage: over 100 gigawatts of onsite gas is planned for data centers, more than a third of new global gas capacity, even as interconnection queues (ERCOT exceeding 230 gigawatts) and social-license backlash bite. One installed-base weakness is self-inflicted — AI-researcher inflow to the United States has dropped about 89% since 2017, eroding a key source of its lead even as private investment ($285.9 billion in 2025) dwarfs rivals.

## Core Interests

- preserve technological and ecosystem advantage
- reduce strategic instability in AI rivalry
- maintain leverage through infrastructure, export controls, and cloud access
- secure the energy, datacenter, and mineral base required for large-scale AI buildout
- close mineral-refining dependence through stockpiling, allied diversification, and hemispheric supply
- shape global standards and open-model ecosystems in U.S.-favorable ways
- engage China selectively on safety without diffusing capabilities
- retain Gulf and wider energy-finance support for U.S.-aligned AI infrastructure expansion
- protect the talent and research installed base underpinning frontier leadership
- preserve human control in nuclear-related AI systems while still integrating AI into broader defense capabilities
