 AI Compute Sovereignty: Infrastructure Control Across Territories, Cloud
Providers, and Accelerators

Zoe Jay Hawkins, Tech Policy Design Institute & Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford
Vili Lehdonvirta, Department of Computer Science, Aalto University & Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford
Boxi Wu, Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford

Abstract: The concept of ‘compute sovereignty’ has become a focal point in government and industry discussions
on artificial intelligence (AI) governance. What is it and who has it? Based on previous literature, we propose to
break these questions down to three levels: (1) how much AI compute a country has on its territory, (2) what is the
nationality of the companies who own the AI compute data centres, and (3) what is the nationality of the accelerator
vendors whose chips power the AI compute data centres? We examine these questions empirically through the lens
of cloud computing infrastructure, focusing on nine leading public cloud providers that represent approximately 70
percent of the global market. The data is collected using a methodology previously published in Lehdonvirta, Wu,
and Hawkins (2024). The findings suggest that the possession of “compute sovereignty” varies between countries
depending on the level of analysis. Determining the most relevant level depends on governments’ policy aims and
national contexts, and involves policy trade-offs. Policies aimed at attracting data centres to a country’s territory can
enhance supply security of critical computational resources while also introducing increased consumption of energy,
water and land use resources, with corresponding localised socioeconomic and environmental impacts. Regional and
supply chain approaches involve different trade-offs.

1. Introduction
In a time of increasing geopolitical competition and uncertainty, the location, ownership and control of artificial
intelligence (AI) technologies and their underlying material infrastructures have become strategic considerations for
governments (Wang and Chen, 2018; Miller, 2022). AI compute – the specialised computational resources that are
required to train and run AI models – has emerged as a sought-after commodity in a global race for AI leadership.
The amount of compute required to train frontier AI models has doubled approximately every six months (Sevilla et
al, 2022), driven by continued pursuit of computational scaling of AI performance (Pilz et al., 2025). For example,
progress in advanced reasoning capabilities in models such as OpenAI’s o3 or DeepSeek’s R1 have relied on
continued computational scaling during inference time. NVIDIA CEO Jenson Huang claimed that “the amount of
computation we need as a result of agentic AI, as a result of reasoning, is easily 100 times more than we thought we
needed this time last year” (Nellis & Cherney, 2025). The perceived need to secure sufficient access to AI compute
for domestic firms, public sector organizations, and/or researchers has come to be referred to using such terms as
“sovereign AI compute” or “compute sovereignty” (Ghahramani, 2023; Sastry et al., 2024; Lehdonvirta et al., 2024;
Government of Canada, 2025). As policymakers seek to assert regulatory authority, ensure supply chain resilience,
and protect strategic autonomy, ‘compute sovereignty’ is becoming a topic of interest in debates about AI
governance (Sastry et al., 2024).
Sovereignty – understood broadly as a government’s supreme authority within a given territory – has a
complex and contested relationship with digital technologies (Pohle & Thiel, 2020). Policies pursuing digital
sovereignty have evolved significantly over the last three decades and particularly since the early Internet debates of
the 1990s. Different stakeholders hold differing views on digital sovereignty; from autocratic arguments regarding
the primacy of the nation state in Internet governance (as opposed to multistakeholder approaches), through to
nations’ increasing pursuit of autonomy from foreign government interference (Musiani, 2024). In western
democracies, digital sovereignty has recently been conceptualised through the lens of national or regional strategic
autonomy in the digital realm (Blancato, 2024).
Amid rising competition to develop advanced AI systems and the corresponding scarcity of computational
resources, notions of ‘digital sovereignty’ are increasing entwined with debates about the governance of AI and the
emerging paradigm of compute governance (Roberts et al., 2023; Sastry et al., 2024; Lehdonvirta et al., 2024). Since
2022, almost all western democratic countries have developed policies and initiatives to increase strategic autonomy
over computational resources, including a turn to industrial policies that aim to onshore the compute supply chain. In
doing so, governments are assessing their dependence on corporate and state actors who own or control key nodes in
the vast, global supply chain that characterises AI compute; from the owners of AI data and models, to the producers

of valuable AI accelerators and the developers and operators of the data centres that house accelerators. Through
these assessments – such as the UK’s Compute Review – countries seek ‘resilience’ as well as concepts of
‘sovereignty’ (Ghahramani, 2023).
And yet, the conceptualisation, trends, and merit of the concept of ‘compute sovereignty’ have been
comparatively under researched, nor analysed for their implications for the governance of AI. To contribute to the
literature, this study: 1) situates the concept of ‘compute sovereignty’ in the broader scholarly context of
‘sovereignty’, ‘digital sovereignty’, and the geopolitics of AI; 2) offers a three-level framework for conceptualising
compute sovereignty; and 3) provides an initial evidence base drawn from a unique census of the global AI compute
infrastructures of nine leading public cloud computing providers based in the United States, Europe, and China,
including Amazon Web Services (AWS), OVHcloud, and Alibaba Cloud. The term 'public AI compute' refers to
cloud-based compute infrastructure made commercially available to users, as opposed to public sector cloud. For
many AI model developers and users, AI compute is primarily accessed via the cloud computing platforms of these
providers, making them a powerful empirical entry point into the phenomenon. They are not the only sources of AI
compute in many countries, so the picture they provide is incomplete, but nevertheless valuable in illustrating how
the question of which countries possess ‘compute sovereignty’ depends on which level of analysis we choose.
The remainder of the study proceeds as follows: a literature review of digital sovereignty and existing
concepts of compute sovereignty; the methodology for conducting a global census of major providers of public AI
compute; discussion of the findings’ implications for national policy considerations, international relations (IR), and
global tech trends.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Digital sovereignty debates
In the fields of political science and IR, sovereignty is a complex and well-studied concept, which has evolved from
16th century notions of monarchical sovereignty to contemporary associations with democracy, the rule of law and
territoriality, often emphasising the importance of sovereignty as geographically bound to a specific territory (Pohle
& Thiel, 2020). Within this literature, sovereignty is discussed through the subvariants of international legal
sovereignty, Westphalian sovereignty, domestic sovereignty, interdependence sovereignty, or formal and operational
sovereignty (Krasner, 1999; Mueller, 2010). Applied to digital sovereignty, debates focus on a national
governments’ ability to deploy and regulate technology within its borders, reflecting a broadly Westphalian notion
of sovereignty.
For cyber libertarians, including prominent American proponents, such as John Barlow, the digital realm is
happily beyond the reach of state power and should remain outside of jurisdictional control (Barlow, 1996; Hawkins
& Weaver, 2025). For most of the last few decades, liberal democratic governments held up this more collaborative
approach between governments and other stakeholders as a strength and intrinsic value of the Internet. In contrast,
the importance of ‘sovereignty’ was the calling card of autocratic governments such as China and Russia seeking
greater state control and pushing back on the United States’ multistakeholder vision of the Internet, where the state
was just one of several stakeholders that had a role in governing the Internet (Hofmann, 2016; Raymond &
DeNardis, 2015; Komaitis et al., 2024). The distinction between these positions was relatively bright and in the
United States, where the notion of digital sovereignty has principally had a negative connotation (Couture & Toupin,
2019).
Since then, this relatively clear distinction has broken down. In the 2010s, the term took on new meaning
following the Edward Snowden revelations, where it was adopted by European governments as they sought to
reduce dependence on American digital networks to reduce risk of foreign surveillance (Hill, 2014). Democracies
now invoke the concept of digital sovereignty in different ways, adding further conceptual blurriness to the term’s
meaning (Musiani, 2024). As geopolitical tensions between the US and China have risen, many ‘third countries’
have found themselves considering what digital sovereignty looks like in the receipt of cloud investments from these
two digital superpowers (Lehdonvirta et al, 2025). With the rise of Big Tech corporate power, the incentive for
governments to assert their sovereignty has expanded from just pushing back on foreign governments but also to
manage the influence of private companies (van Dijck, 2020). The perceived agency of Big Tech is such that
scholars have also explored the meaning of ‘digital sovereignty’ as applicable to private corporations (Floridi, 2020;
Lehdonvirta, 2022). Concurrently, the supply chain shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic intensified national
conversations about dependence on international supply chains and the pursuit of resilient infrastructure. As such,

the pervasiveness and criticality of digital infrastructure to modern state, economy and society has increased, along
with growing interest in policies that evoke digital sovereignty.
2.2 Compute Sovereignty
Debates about ‘digital sovereignty’ and its meaning in academic and policy discourse have most recently
been impacted by the huge interest in AI. Many governments see AI as a potential source of national economic,
military, and/or cultural advantage (Wang and Chen 2018; Miller 2022), and have consequently adopted policies
aimed at securing sufficient access to AI compute resources for local industries and researchers (Vipra and West
2023). The term ‘compute sovereignty’ and adjacent terms such as ‘sovereign AI compute’ have emerged as
something of a subcategory of digital sovereignty to describe this perceived need to secure access to AI compute
resources (Ghahramani, 2023; Sastry et al., 2024; Lehdonvirta et al., 2024; Government of Canada, 2025).
Compute sovereignty has been pursued through industrial policies to onshore components of the compute
supply chain. The US CHIPS Act (2022) authorised 39 billion USD in grants and loans, alongside tax credits and
government research and development investments into US chip manufacturing, while the European Chips Act
(2023) proposes to invest 43 billion euros of public funds up to 2030 to ensure Europe’s ‘strategic autonomy’ in the
supply of critical chips including a Chips Fund to support European chip production including the onshoring of
foundries (Shivakumar et al., 2024). These policies are often reliant on multi-billion dollar compute investments
from hyperscale cloud providers who in practice deploy the AI accelerator chips in their data centres and make them
accessible to developers and users. In 2025 alone, Microsoft plans to spend $80 billion USD on global AI data
centre buildouts (Smith, 2025). These investments are welcomed by governments who, through initiatives such as
the UK’s AI Opportunities Act (2025), propose access to land and cheaper energy, as well as fast-tracked
development permits for data centre developers.
Other approaches to compute sovereignty have involved the development of public sector compute
resources. Both the UK Compute Review (2023) and the Canadian Sovereign AI Compute Strategy (2025) propose
investments into public sector supercomputing infrastructure to bolster domestic compute availability. This trend is
likely to continue, with forecasts suggesting that worldwide ‘sovereign cloud’ spending will reach more than $250
billion in 2027 (IDC, 2023). This global rush for ‘sovereign AI’ has been described by scholars as ‘the 21st century’s
arms race’ (Pratschke, 2025).
Like the conceptual blurriness of digital sovereignty, compute sovereignty has different meanings for
different actors. Defining it is complicated by the complexities of the AI compute supply chain, which is highly
international and characterised by significant market concentration and vertical integration at different steps (Vipra
& West, 2023; Beaumier & Cartwright, 2024). In very broad terms, the supply chain consists of companies that
consume AI compute to develop and/or deploy AI systems; companies that own data centres that supply that AI
compute; and companies that design and fabricate AI accelerators housed in those data centres (Sastry et al., 2024).
Several of these links are characterised by intense political and economic interdependence which governments can
weaponise as a geoeconomic tool to attempt to exert influence over other countries (Farrell & Newman, 2019;
Beaumier & Cartwright, 2024, Lehdonvirta et al., 2025).
In the following sections, we propose to distinguish between three different understandings or ‘levels’ of
AI compute sovereignty discussed in previous academic literature and policy documents: compute sovereignty as
jurisdiction over (1) data centres, (2) companies that own the data centres, and (3) companies that provide the AI
chips that power the data centres.

2.3 Compute sovereignty as territorial jurisdiction over AI compute data centres

The broadest interpretation of compute sovereignty is having AI compute resources physically situated
within the borders of the government’s territory, referred to as ‘in-country compute’ (OECD, 2025). This conception
is based on the Westphalian sovereign state model which links a government’s supreme authority to govern with a
defined physical territory (Krasner, 2001). Governments that exercise territorial jurisdiction over physical compute
infrastructures have the opportunity to regulate them in a way that other governments cannot (Sastry et al., 2024;
Ferrari, 2024). Having territorial jurisdiction over AI compute data centres empowers governments for instance to
enforce Know-Your-Customer checks, audits, or other controls at the point at which AI models and data sets enter a
data centre for training or deployment (Sastry et al., 2024). For this reason, for some governments there is a

4

motivation to increase the territorial nexus with foreign firms providing AI compute cloud computing services.
Countries attempt to shore up their territorial claims over in-country compute through both inducement (investment
incentives) and regulation (such as data localisation laws).

In practice, multinational corporations also embrace this territorial interpretation of ‘sovereignty’. Adopting
this definition means that they can offer ‘sovereign’ digital services to foreign governments simply by situating
sufficient proportions of their infrastructure, business functions, staff, and/or other assets within the host country’s
geographic borders. Microsoft offers its Microsoft Cloud for Sovereignty program which ‘provide the necessary
control without trading off cloud value’ (Microsoft, 2024). The AWS Digital Sovereignty Pledge offers a
‘commitment to offering AWS customers the most advanced set of sovereignty controls and features available in the
cloud’ (AWS, 2022). Similarly, Huawei’s National Government Cloud Solution claims that its ‘on-premise
deployment ensures digital sovereignty’ in China (Huawei, n.d).

2.4 Compute sovereignty as the nationality of the AI compute cloud providers

For some policy makers and local competitors to foreign cloud providers, AI compute being located within one’s
territory provides insufficient sovereign control over compute resources (Michels, 2025). In this view, the
nationality of the cloud provider managing the provision of AI compute from the data centres within one’s territory
is consequential. This ownership dynamic can be framed as determining both a ‘freedom to’ and ‘freedom from’
perspective. Firstly, possessing capability and capacity to provide computational resources through locally
domiciled organisations is seen as a national advantage for economic and security reasons – a form of technological
self-sufficiency towards ‘innovation power’ (Schmidt, 2023). Secondly, and just as importantly, this approach is
perceived to offer the benefit of preventing a foreign government’s digital control over critical infrastructure. In a
post-Snowden world, some are not comforted by AI compute facilities being located within their own jurisdiction if
the cloud provider is a foreign company.
In this case, the interests of large corporations and their home governments are assumed by some to be aligned
to the extent that corporate expansion represents a form of ‘digital colonialism’ where global reliance on their digital
infrastructure can be weaponized by their home government (Kwet, 2019; Farrell & Newman, 2019). Even for those
who do not suspect intentional state-corporate partnership, there is a remaining concern that a corporation may be
forced to comply with foreign government direction. This could relate to a home government’s opportunity to
engage in surveillance, where “a provider who is subject to foreign jurisdiction might disclose data to a foreign
government without a customer’s knowledge or permission” (Michels, 2025). Others point to the long arm of US
legislation - including the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act 1978 and Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data
Act 2018 - as posing risks of jurisdictional reach into US companies. These concerns are reflected in ‘trusted vendor’
frameworks, adopted by governments to mitigate the perceived risks of Chinese government interference in Chinese
owned digital infrastructure such as 5G networks (Botton & Lee-Makiyama, 2018). Another way of pursuing
immunity from foreign laws is the European Commission’s decision to preclude majority foreign-owned
corporations from receiving the highest certification under the bloc’s cloud security standard, modelled after
France’s SecNumCloud.
In practice, compute sovereignty based on provider nationality is a challenging goal for most countries,
particularly those already dependent on foreign compute providers. Given the intensely concentrated global cloud
market - dominated by six hyperscale cloud providers (US corporations AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure,
and Chinese corporations Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud and Tencent Cloud) – cultivating locally-owned
competitors with the capital and scale to meaningfully compete the global giants is no small feat (Lehdonvirta et al.,
2024, 2025). As a result, in practical terms, most countries must engage in strategic trade-offs between digital
infrastructure investments from US or Chinese cloud providers (Lehdonvirta et al., 2025).
While the global distribution of AI compute resources discussed in this study are a product of the investment
patters of private corporations, these corporate decisions are increasingly informed and constrained by government
legislation and policy narratives around the world that frame access to - and governance of - AI compute resources
as a national security issue, and the race for AI dominance between the US and China as a an international security
issue. In this context, we can borrow from IR theories of great power competition, to view the global distribution of
US versus Chinese owned AI compute cloud providers as resulting in individual countries being in a position of
either ‘hedging’ or ‘aligning’ their compute interests in the context of US-China competition. In IR theory,
‘hedging’ refers to a mixed strategy deployed by a country to avoid ‘picking sides’ between competing powers

5

(Goh, 2005, 2007). According to hedging theory, countries that do not want to choose between the US and China
‘hedge’ relations and refuse a specific alignment (Goh, 2005). Much of the literature focuses on the strategies of
countries in the Asia-Pacific and their relations with the US or China (Ciorciari and Haacke, 2019). In contrast,
some countries pick sides by aligning with a powerful state, either to oppose the threatening rise of another
(‘balancing’) (Waltz, 1979), or to join a threatening power to avoid the costs of opposing it (‘bandwagoning’) (Walt,
1987). Regardless of intent, both result in a country ‘aligning’ with a particular powerful state. These categories of
‘hedging’ and ‘aligning’ can be applied as a metaphor to describe third countries’ situations when it comes to the
nationality of the cloud providers that operate AI compute infrastructure on their territories—are they of a single
foreign nationality (aligning) or of multiple (hedging).

2.5 Compute sovereignty as AI compute accelerator vendor nationality

The third, and most granular conception of compute sovereignty centres on the nationality of the providers of AI
accelerators. Even if data centres are located within a country’s territorial jurisdiction, and owned by a company
headquartered in that country, their ability to continue to provide compute relies on the supply of advanced AI
accelerators. In this framing, compute sovereignty is impacted by the major suppliers of this hardware: the US chip
design firm NVIDIA, and US and Chinese hyperscale cloud providers.
Accelerators are made from semiconductor materials which make the supply of accelerators heavily dependent
on the semiconductor supply chain. This supply chain is highly concentrated and consists of many steps where
control could be exercised; to simplify, the main steps are design, fabrication and distribution, with key processes
distributed across centralised nodes (Sastry et al., 2024; Thadani & Allen, 2023). The US design firm NVIDIA
designs 80-95% of chips with Intel and AMD being marginal competitors, while 90% of semiconductor fabrication
occurs in Taiwan by the Taiwan Semiconductor Company (TSMC) which is in turn entirely reliant on lithography
equipment supplied by the Dutch ASML company (Miller, 2022). Finally, chips are distributed via NVIDIA to
compute providers such as AWS, Google and Microsoft Azure who make up over 70% of the global cloud compute
market. Although chip fabrication is largely outsourced to foundries such as TSMC, the vendors retain licensing
rights and often embed export restrictions into their supply chain contracts. Moreover, TSMC does not have design
capabilities, meaning that the US, as the home-state of leading vendor NVIDIA, has notable control over the supply
chain (Beaumier & Cartwright, 2024). However, this supply chain has long been entwined with geopolitical tensions
and concerns about the insecurity of this concentrated global supply chain (noting the fragility of the ‘Silicon Shield’
argument that Taiwan’s role in the semiconductor supply chain elevates its importance to the US and deters Chinese
aggression (Weil et al., 2024)). The major US and Chinese hyperscale cloud providers have all increased
investments into in-house accelerators in an attempt to compete and diversity the market.
The emergence of in-house accelerators has been intensified by the geopolitical tensions by export controls
seeking to shape the global accelerator market by restricting China’s access to US accelerators. The US
Government's CHIPS Act (2022) prevents NVIDIA from selling its most advanced accelerators to China with the
intention to prevent cloud providers and other data centre operators in China from building significant AI compute
resources (Allen, 2024). While the policy appears to have been successful in diminishing Chinese developers’ access
to AI compute in the near term, it has incentivised Chinese technology firms and the government to invest into
sovereign AI accelerator design and fabrication capabilities. The leading public cloud provider Huawei, recently
partnered with SMIC to develop an advanced semiconductor (a 7nm chip) with capabilities that would overwise
have been prohibited within US export controls (Gupta et al., 2024). Alibaba and Tencent, have likewise also
announced their own AI accelerator chip development programmes.
As with hyperscale public cloud computing, it is hardly realistic for most countries to move to autarky and
rapidly develop a domestic AI accelerator industry to secure ‘compute sovereignty’ for themselves (Beaumier &
Cartwright, 2024). Instead, any active attempts by governments to ensure supply security and avoid dependence on
this level would revolve around ‘friendshoring’ (Maihold, 2022) or choosing which foreign AI accelerator chip
vendors to depend on. As on the previous level, we can conceptualize countries’ situation in this regard as either
‘hedging’ or ‘aligning’, depending on whether they rely on chips from multiple nationalities or just one.

3. Methodology: Census of Public Cloud Regions
Our methodology builds on prior research mapping the political geography of US and Chinese hyperscale public
cloud providers through the lens of data centres and AI accelerators (Lehdonvirta et al., 2024, 2025). In this study,
we conducted a census of US, Chinese and European public cloud providers’ cloud region (defined below) and the
most powerful AI-relevant accelerators available in each region.
Following the census, we analysed the geographic distribution of regions where customers can access US
or Chinese AI accelerators. Our census comprised three leading cloud providers in each of the US, Chinese and
European markets considering both representation from major economic markets, as well as the relative market
share of each provider. The US, Chinese and European markets capture approximately 78% of global spending on
cloud infrastructure (Zhang, 2024), while US and Chinese providers represent the majority of the global cloud
market (Ledonvirta et al., 2025). Although European cloud providers are not significant global players they have a
non-negligible role in their region, particularly as European countries pursue compute sovereignty policies that aim
to support European providers. The census therefore included AWS, Google and Microsoft (US providers), Alibaba,
Huawei and Tencent (Chinese providers), and Exoscale, Hetzner and OVHCloud (European providers) (Table 1).

Using publicly available data accessed via cloud providers’ websites and customer interfaces we collect a
list of each provider’s public cloud regions as of November 2024. A ‘cloud region’ is an organisational designation
for a cluster of interconnected data centres called ‘availability zones’, which are located in a specific geographic
location and named after a nearby city. A developer wishing to purchase on-demand or subscription-based AI
compute would therefore choose the region where their desired type of accelerator is accessible and physically
hosted. The chosen coverage of accelerators is based on those considered the most powerful and relevant in the
training and inferencing of common AI models in 2024 (Table 2). The accelerators covered are those made available
to the public via hyperscale cloud provider’s platforms, which comprise NVIDIA accelerators as well as provider’s
in-house accelerators. This covers a significant portion of the total AI accelerator market because NVIDIA chips
make up roughly 80-95% of the total chip market with US hyperscale cloud providers alone make up 40% of
NVIDIA’s revenue (Sastry et al., 2024).
We have categorised these accelerators into those relevant for both frontier model training as well as
inferencing, and those suited predominantly to inferencing. When choosing accelerators, developers will consider
the computational needs of the project, as well as cost and availability. Training and inferencing accelerators
(‘training-relevant’) are typically the most power and expensive GPUs (i.e. NVIDIA’s A100 or H100) required to
train advanced models because they are more computationally intensive (demanding high compute bandwidth).
GPUs like the NVIDIA H100 are typically purchased by labs that can afford them, for their training abilities,
although they are still considered the optimal GPU for both training and inferencing (Patel & Nishball, 2023). In

7

contrast, accelerators that are typically used for inferencing (‘inferencing-relevant’), like NVIDIA’s V100, are often
more memory intensive for higher throughput (demanding more memory bandwidth).
As smaller, more efficient models enter the market, such as DeepSeek’s R1 or OpenAI’s o3-mini, the
distinction between training-relevant compute and inferencing-relevant compute has been drawn into question.
However, researchers argue that the current paradigm of AI development is still focused on large-scale frontier
models; with many smaller models distilled from larger ones and reliant on them for data generation, evaluation and
continued improvement (Pilz et al., 2025). Therefore, the purpose of our categorisation is to separate today’s most
powerful accelerators that are best suited for intensive workloads to train cutting edge frontier models, from other
accelerators which – while theoretically possible to use for certain training - are fundamentally less powerful. A

By focusing our census on public cloud providers, we focus on sovereignty as it relates to compute which is
publicly available through purchase or subscription via a provider’s cloud platform. It is worth clarifying that this
compute is ‘public’ because of its availability to the public and not because of any government affiliation (i.e. the
public in public house, rather than in public sector) (Lehdonvirta et al., 2024). As such, the study excludes private

4. Findings
4.1 Compute sovereignty as territorial jurisdiction over AI compute data centres
A major way in which “compute sovereignty” has been understood is having data centres running AI accelerators
situated within a country’s territorial jurisdiction. Under this definition, which countries appear “compute sovereign”
in the public cloud AI compute census data set?
The census discovered 225 cloud regions situated on the territories of 43 different countries. A cloud region
is in simple terms a cluster of large, interconnected data centres situated roughly in the same area. Of these 225
cloud regions, 132 were found to be equipped with AI accelerators. These 132 accelerator-enabled cloud regions
were situated in 33 different countries. In other words, a total of 33 countries had some amount of public cloud AI
compute capacity situated within their territorial jurisdictions. Most countries in the world had none. These public
cloud AI compute host countries are predominantly located in North America, Europe, Middle East, and Asia
(Table 4). In South America, only Brazil hosts public cloud AI compute on its territory, while in Africa only South
Africa does so.
A further distinction may be made between cloud regions that are equipped with accelerators that are
considered powerful enough for both training and inferencing or just inferencing new frontier AI models. The
census revealed that a total of 24 of those countries hosted public cloud AI training-relevant compute, while 9 of the
33 countries hosted only inference-relevant AI compute. The remaining countries in the world have no public cloud
AI compute according to our census of nine leading providers (Table 4). Individual regions can host multiple chip
types, therefore the count of regions with training-relevant chips and regions with inference-relevant chips are not
mutually exclusive i.e. some regions will be counted in both tallies. For example, Korea has a total of 4 compute
regions. Of these 4, 3 are regions with training-relevant chips while 2 are regions with inference-relevant chips i.e.
one of those regions contains both training-relevant and inference-relevant chips.

4.2 Compute sovereignty as AI compute provider nationality
The second way in which ‘compute sovereignty’ has been understood is by reference to the nationality of the cloud
providers and other firms and organisations that operate AI data centres. The highest level of compute sovereignty
from this point of view is achieved when data centres are not only situated within the country’s territory but also
operated by domestically owned cloud provider firms. Under this definition, only the US, China, and a handful of
European countries qualify as compute sovereign in the census data set. This result partly reflects the fact that the
global market for the provision of public AI compute is dominated by a small number of large providers, which are
the subject of our census. However, smaller, local cloud providers not captured by the census may nevertheless
make meaningful contributions to their home markets’ AI compute capacity. Therefore, we cannot use the census
data to make definitive conclusions about every country’s compute sovereignty status in regards to the existence of
national compute providers.
That being said, while some countries do have their own strong and growing market of locally owned
public AI compute providers, the hyperscalers remain dominant across the global cloud market (Lehdonvirta et al.,
2024, 2025). Therefore, the ownership and nationality of the foreign companies that provide this AI compute, is a
relevant consideration for governments when thinking about compute sovereignty. In lieu of AI compute made
available by domestically owned cloud providers, whether the oversight and guarantee of these services is aligned to
one foreign power, or split between multiple, has implications for a nation’s strategic calculus. Using our census, we
compare the presence of foreign providers in each country to ask: are countries hedging their international digital
infrastructure dependency between providers from more than one foreign country, or aligning with providers from a
single foreign country only?
In total, 18 countries relied on providers from just one of either China or the US, while 12 countries hosted
providers of multiple nationalities (Table 5). The US and China themselves are not sorted into these categories but
included in the table for context and completeness. Austria is the only country that has no foreign AI compute,
relying solely on European providers.

4.3 Compute sovereignty as AI accelerator chip vendor nationality
The third way in which “compute sovereignty” has been understood is by reference to control over AI accelerator
chip supply chains. Governments that are able to exercise control over chip supply chains are able to secure
continued supply for their own domestic providers and data centres, whilst potentially denying access to rivals’.
Which countries have “compute sovereignty” under this definition?
The US company NVIDIA are by far the largest seller of AI accelerators to cloud providers by market
share, comprising 80-95% of the total chip market (Sastry et al., 2024). But over the past two years, some Chinese
hyperscale cloud providers have introduced their own in-house AI accelerator chip models. Of the 132 accelerator-
enabled regions in the census, only six offer Huawei Ascend accelerators, with three regions in China and three
regions in France, Singapore and the UAE. This means 95.5% of accelerator-enabled regions in the census are
powered by US-owned accelerators. These results reflect US, and particularly NVIDIA’s, immense market
concentration. As a result, in the census, only the US and China host AI accelerators sourced from domestic chip
vendors. They are therefore also the only countries hosting public AI compute data centres operated by domestic
vendors equipped with at least some domestically designed chips. Outside of France, Singapore and the UAE, the

12

remaining AI compute host countries’ accelerators were all sourced from vendors of a single foreign nationality,
namely the United States. In the terminology adopted earlier, the vast majority of countries were ‘aligned’ with the
US on this level of compute sovereignty.

5. Discussion
Our analysis of data on public cloud AI compute reveals that a country’s ‘compute sovereignty’ status varies
depending on which of multiple understandings or ‘levels’ of compute sovereignty is used. This section discusses
the findings of each level of analysis in turn and draws implications to future research and policy.
5.1 Territorial Sovereignty: Do you have (or want) AI compute in-country?
Territorial compute sovereignty, or the possession of ‘in-country compute’, is the most widely held type of compute
sovereignty around the world, as judged through the lens of public cloud AI compute, a large and important segment
of AI compute. Even still, only a minority of countries worldwide (33) qualified as having at least inference-relevant
AI compute within their jurisdiction, and only 24 of those countries also have training-relevant AI compute within
their jurisdiction. That is about 17% and 12% of UN member states respectively. This concentration of in-country
access in such a small number of places reveals the global disparity in distribution of this strategic resource around
the world between the ‘compute haves and have nots’ (Lehdonvirta et al., 2024).
However, should every government want AI compute on its territory? As outlined in the literature review,
the possible strategic benefits that motivate the ‘compute sovereignty’ discourse include security of supply to what
is perceived to be a critical resource, and also the ability to use control over that resource to regulate AI applications
and possibly also to deny access to strategic rivals (Beaumier & Cartwright, 2024). Besides any possible strategic
benefits that ‘compute sovereignty’ brings, governments also see economic benefits in large digital infrastructures.
Building data centres housing AI compute brings with it tax revenue and local employment, initially for
construction, followed later by management and operation of the data centre (TechUK, 2024).
That being said, there is also increasing attention on the possible downsides of hosting AI data centres
within a jurisdiction. The economic benefits of data centres, usually deriving from economic impact assessments
conducted by Big Tech corporations or data centre industry bodies, have been challenged by researchers and civil

13

society advocacy groups. Data centres require high upfront capital investment despite having low labour intensity,
and thus has a relatively limited value add compared to other digital industries. Data centre developers may be given
tax breaks or subsidies from local governments, which may offset their tax revenue contributions (Whiton, 2023). AI
data centres consume large amounts of electricity, causing upward pressure on electricity prices for alternative
industrial users and consumers especially in places where electricity supply is fairly inelastic (Judge, 2022). Data
centres can also require vast quantities of water to operate, with disproportionate extractive dynamics for
geographies experiencing ongoing droughts (Monserrate, 2022).
From a purely economic perspective, investing into developing national data centre industries may only pay
off for countries that have resources that make them particularly competitive in this industry, such as cooler climates
and high-capacity electricity infrastructure serving low-cost, low-carbon power, and access to plentiful cooling
water. Moreover, community and civil society groups can dispute data centre developments that impact on their
local areas (Rone, 2022, 2024). Such political costs can make data centre promotion politically unattractive
especially for local governments responsible for zoning but not for foreign policy or national security. The pursuit of
territorial AI compute sovereignty thus involves trade-offs between competing priorities. As a result, the correct
policy to pursue will depend on the specific strategic circumstances of each country.

An alternative to purely national ‘in-country’ AI compute sovereignty are attempts to achieve compute
sovereignty through regional frameworks of trust and collective governance. For example, the European High
Performance Computing Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC) is a public-private partnership established in 2018 by the
European Union, participating European countries, and private partners. It represents an effort by EU member states
to pool resources and coordinate the development and use of high-performance computing infrastructure across
member state borders, allowing countries to benefit from shared compute capacity while retaining a degree of
influence over its governance. Among other facilities it has built LUMI, a sizeable AI compute cluster located in
Kajaani, Finland. Such frameworks can offer smaller or less resource-rich states access to advanced compute
infrastructure without requiring them to host it domestically. They also distribute economic and environmental costs
more evenly and can support common regulatory frameworks for the responsible use of AI. However, regional

14

approaches require high levels of political trust and institutional coordination and may still fall short of the full
control that some governments seek when invoking ‘sovereignty.’ Thus, compute sovereignty can be seen not as a
binary condition but as a spectrum of arrangements—ranging from fully national control to shared regional
governance—each carrying distinct political, economic, and environmental implications.
5.2 Provider Sovereignty: Does it matter who owns your AI compute data centres?
While regionalism can expand the idea of jurisdiction outward, a second challenge emerges from the inward pull of
foreign legal authority. When foreign hyperscalers operate AI compute within a host country, they bring with them
the regulatory reach of their home governments, creating a layered or overlapping jurisdiction. This can result in
conflicted compliance obligations for private sector cloud providers and diminished regulatory exclusivity for host
states. For example, a China-headquartered cloud provider operating in Malaysia may be fully subject to Malaysian
law, but it is also bound by Chinese legal instruments such as the National Security Law 2018, which allows
Chinese authorities to demand access to data stored abroad by Chinese firms. This creates overlapping sovereign
claims that can result in conflicting legal obligations on the cloud providers (Abraha, 2019). As cloud services are
integrated into sensitive sectors such as defence, health and critical infrastructure, this multilayered jurisdiction
raises concerns for some that location without control is not sovereignty.
When applying the second level of our AI compute sovereignty framework, we find that, excluding the US
and China, 12 countries (or roughly one third of the 31other countries with accelerator enabled regions) are hedging
between cloud providers from the US and China, while 18 (or just under two thirds) are aligning with cloud
providers from just one of these superpowers. Of those aligned countries, 5 are aligned with Chinese digital
infrastructure, and 13 aligned with US digital infrastructure.
The decision between hedging or aligning between competing superpowers is a well-established strategic
concept in international relations and international security studies. As with the previous level, what constitutes
‘sovereignty’ at this level is for most countries, in reality, not so black and white. If AI compute sovereignty is
interpreted as control over who operates and governs the cloud infrastructure supplying high-performance compute,
then sovereignty becomes a question not only of domestic ownership, but of strategic alignment with foreign
providers. In the absence of domestic hyperscalers with the capability and capacity to support economy wide access
to public AI compute, many governments face the strategic choice: are they hedging between superpowers with
digital infrastructure from multiple foreign cloud providers; or are the aligning with the infrastructure of one
superpower?
Sovereignty through AI compute alignment – relying on a single foreign ally: Some states can be seen to be
aligning by selecting a single foreign provider, typically from an allied country, and deepening that relationship to
ensure reliable access to high-performance AI compute. These arrangements often reflect broader political or
defence alignments, where a foreign cloud provider is seen not merely as a commercial actor bus as an extension of
a trusted state’s technology ecosystem (Lehdonvirta et al., 2025). For example, the Australian Government has
partnered with AWS to establish a Top-Secret Cloud for Australia’s national intelligence community – the
government’s most sensitive data (Australian Government, 2024). Looking at the census findings, other countries
including Japan, and Israel are aligning with US cloud providers, while countries including Chile, Indonesia and
Saudi Arabia are aligning exclusively with Chinese cloud providers. Through this lens, a type of sovereignty can be
exercised through selective interdependence with an ally perceived to be politically stable, technologically advanced
and ideologically aligned (Adler-Nissen, 2008).

15

Sovereignty through AI compute hedging: multiple providers for resilience: Other countries’ AI compute
dependence is distributed across multiple foreign cloud providers. Like balancing, this approach does not offer
sovereignty through self-reliance but strategic autonomy through diversification. Evelyn Goh describes this as a
strategy, which is common across countries in Southeast Asia, to ‘cultivate a middle position that forestalls or avoids
having to choose one side at the obvious expense of the other’ (Goh, 2005, 2). For example, in this census,
Singapore hosts 3 AI compute regions from US cloud providers and 3 AI compute regions from Chinese cloud
providers. Conceptually, this approach can be seen as a response to the weaponized interdependence theory, which
warns against being held captive by one single foreign power (Farrell & Newman, 2019).
These distinct profiles – aligning and hedging – suggest that AI compute sovereignty is not a black and
white one-size-fits all goal, but a strategic continuum shaped by risk appetite, trust relations, and geopolitical
alignment. For many mid-size and non-aligned states, the pursuit of AI compute sovereignty may not involve full-
self-sufficiency or domestic control but navigating dependencies in a way that simultaneously preserves autonomy
and reduces vulnerability.
5.3 Accelerator Sovereignty: Do you care about the nationality of your AI accelerator vendor?
The most technologically foundational – and geopolitically constrained – layer of AI compute sovereignty concerns
the ownership and control of the AI accelerator vendors that design and distribute the chips required for training and
deployment of AI models. Like the previous level of our framework, extreme concentration in the global accelerator
vendor market means that only the US and China have regions powered accelerators from domestic vendors.
However, given the comparative market lead NVIDIA has over Chinese counterparts in performance, and with
Huawei’s Ascend chips not found in any region outside of China availability, most countries are left without a
meaningful choice but to align with NVIDIA. What does that mean for notions of AI compute sovereignty?
Despite being a foreign-owned provider, NVIDIA positions itself as a key partner to countries around the
world in their pursuit of sovereignty, stating that “Since 2019, NVIDIA’s AI Nations initiative has helped countries
spanning every region of the globe to build sovereign AI capabilities” (Lee, 2024). For example, Israel and the
United Kingdom continue to scale their sovereign AI compute infrastructure investments using NVIDIA hardware.
In contrast, other jurisdictions are looking to diversify their reliance on NVIDIA accelerators by building up
domestic semiconductor capacity. For example, the EU Chips Act 2022 includes a €43 billion investment plan for
domestic semiconductor development (European Commission, 2022). At the same time, the US is seeking to
onshore aspects of TSMC operations to further concentrate US control over accelerator supply chains (US
Department of Commerce, 2024). The full impacts of this policy on the market remain to be seen.
Accelerator vendor level AI compute sovereignty presents perhaps the most complex and long-term
element in our three-level framework. The building of local AI compute data centres and development of domestic
cloud providers takes investment and effort over many years, but the research and development, capital and talent
required for chip design and fabrication, even more so. It is therefore unsurprising, then, that most states pursue AI
compute vendor sovereignty through aligning with US vendor NVIDIA.
6. Conclusion
AI is considered by some governments a core axis of global strategic competition, national security and economic
innovation, and is thus increasingly entwined with questions of digital sovereignty. Underpinning its geostrategic
importance for governments is one foundational input - AI compute – making questions of where AI compute is
located, who owns it and who governs it, central geopolitical questions. This has implications for AI governance as
countries seek to exert control and independence in global and interdependent supply chains.
This study has presented an analytical framework that allows for multi-level examination of sovereignty
across the AI compute supply chain, including: 1) territorial jurisdiction over AI compute infrastructure; 2)
ownership and nationality of cloud providers; and 3) ownership and nationality of AI accelerator chip vendors.
Empirically, the paper presents a global census of public AI compute infrastructure of the world’s three largest
hyperscaler blocs – those based in the US, China, and Europe. The data reveals striking asymmetries: only 34
countries host any public AI compute; only 24 of those have access to training level compute; and most rely on
cloud or chip infrastructure controlled by a small number of foreign actors. There are 18 host countries aligning with
one superpower’s AI cloud compute infrastructure, while 12 hedge by relying on infrastructure from both US and
Chinese owned companies. All countries in the census apart from China rely on US-owned NVIDIA accelerators to

16

power the AI compute data centres in their territories. These insights are not just technological, they are
fundamentally geopolitical.
Importantly, while this paper puts significant effort into understanding and measuring AI compute
sovereignty, this should not be misconstrued as advocating for its pursuit. Territorial AI compute sovereignty
presents a range of benefits and drawbacks, so this strategic question should receive its due consideration,
specificity, and critical analysis from policymakers around the world.
This analysis invites several avenues for further research. First, repeating and expanding a similar census in
the future will offer valuable longitudinal insights into how the global distribution of data centres and leading-edge
AI accelerators unfolds, how sovereignty configurations evolve over time, and whether efforts by some states to
rebalance dependencies are successful. Second, there is a need to explore how sovereignty strategies evolve in
response to changing geopolitical conditions—for example, a second Trump Presidency, with its unpredictable
approach to alliances, growing techno-nationalist policies, may dramatically reshape perceptions of interdependence
and sovereignty, while pushing back on other countries’ attempts to regulate these US companies. Third, future
work should investigate the role of these private sector actors, such as the hyperscalers, shape the legitimacy and
accountability of sovereignty claims in this domain.
As Pohle & Thiel (2020) caution, digital sovereignty is not an end in itself but a political project shaped by
underlying values and power dynamics. This study contributes an empirical and conceptual foundation for
examining what kind of sovereignty is being asserted in the AI era, by whom, and at what cost.
