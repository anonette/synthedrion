The openness paradox:
Open-source AI and China’s quest
for cyber sovereignty
Grace X. Yang
Institute for East European Studies, Freie Universität Berlin
Internet and Communication Policy Center, University of Salerno
Abstract
China’s embrace of open-source AI appears to contradict its centralized “cyber sovereignty” doctrine.
This article analyses China’s open-source AI strategy through a four-layer framework (physical, logical,
application, societal), revealing how openness is repurposed to serve state-centric goals: achieving techno-
logical self-reliance, controlling applications and data, and steering innovation toward state-deﬁned prior-
ities. Globally, the strategy functions as asymmetric competition, countering U.S. tech dominance through
the narrative of “AI democratization,” while shaping industry standards and subtly promoting China’s
vision of digital governance across the world.
Keywords
Open-source AI, China, cyber sovereignty, digital authoritarianism, soft power, Global South
A country known for its strict digital control, China is
now emerging as a global advocate for open-source arti-
ﬁcial intelligence. The rise of the Chinese open-source
AI developer DeepSeek has been called as a “Pearl
Harbor moment”—a sudden and disruptive challenge
to the existing order (Lv, 2025). By delivering top-tier
large language models at a fraction of the cost, it has
triggered a global shift in the AI landscape, challenging
U.S. tech dominance (Lee and Shubhangi, 2025). This
shift reveals a contradiction: open-source values of
freedom, transparency, and decentralization seem at
odds with China’s “cyber sovereignty” doctrine. This
article argues that the contradiction is only apparent, as
China strategically repurposes open-source to reinforce
its “cyber sovereignty” doctrine and digital authoritarian
practices, advance technological self-reliance, and
export its model of digital governance.
The “cyber sovereignty” agenda is a top-down
national policy that has long been put into practice
since China was connected to the internet in the
1990s, marked by the establishment of the “Great
Fire Wall” (Yang, 2025). In the AI ﬁeld, as early
as 2017, the State Council of China had issued
the
“Next
Generation
Artiﬁcial
Intelligence
Development Plan,” clearly stating that by 2030,
China would become a major global center for arti-
ﬁcial intelligence innovation, and build a “compre-

This industrial policy reveals the logic behind

must serve national strategic interests, and open-source

current geopolitical landscape.
China’s cyber sovereignty practices could be
deconstructed through a four-layer framework: the
physical layer, logical layer, application layer, and
societal layer (Liu and Ye, 2020). The physical
layer prioritizes the control of the critical infrastruc-
ture such as chips and servers; the logical layer
focuses on the autonomy of core code and protocols
such as operating systems, software frameworks,
and
technical
standards;
the application layer
focuses on the management of data ﬂows, especially
the cross-border data ﬂows; and the societal layer
focuses on the regulation of online content, platform
activity and user identity through laws and adminis-
trative regulations. China’s open-source AI strategy
has been precisely deployed at these four levels to
serve its ultimate goal of consolidating techno-
logical sovereignty and achieving state control.
At the physical layer, China’s open-source strat-
egy is a direct countermeasure to United States’
hardware
sanctions.
The
guiding
principle
is
“using software to supplement hardware” (以软补
硬),
where
algorithmic
innovation
overcomes
material constraints and limited computing power
(Zheng, 2025). DeepSeek exempliﬁes this approach
by using its MLA architecture and resource-efﬁcient
design to deliver high performance on less powerful
chips, requiring only 1/11 of the training compute
used by Meta’s Llama (Lan, 2025). Other Chinese
AI ﬁrms, such as the speech-recognition giant
iFlytek, have also adopted this strategy, although
sometimes at a cost, as is shown by its three-month
delay after switching from Nvidia to Huawei chips,
to counter United States’ semiconductor and chip
sanctions (Jiang, 2025). The goal is to build a
domestic ecosystem running on domestic chips to
achieve technological self-reliance (Chan et al.,
2025).
At the logical layer, the strategy aims to break
China’s dependency on the U.S. software stack.
China’s AI ecosystem remains heavily reliant on a
U.S.-developed software, including frameworks
such as PyTorch and TensorFlow, projects like
Ollama and NumPy, and code-hosting platforms
like GitHub and Hugging Face (Long et al., 2025).
This dependency means control of the open-source
ecosystem remains outside Beijing’s hands, creating
signiﬁcant strategic risk, and should the proposed
“Decoupling
America’s
Artiﬁcial
Intelligence
Capabilities from China Act of 2025” pass in the
U.S. Congress, China’s AI ecosystem would face
serious risks (Long et al., 2025). Beijing’s response
has been to aggressively promote domestic alterna-
tives like Baidu’s PaddelPaddel and Huawei’s
MindSpace, using open-source models to incentivize
a full-stack domestic migration (Chan et al., 2025).
At the application layer, the core concern is the
control over data ﬂow and usage, particularly per-
sonal, sensitive, and cross-border data. China’s Data
Security Law (2021a) and Personal Information
Protection Law (2021b), mandate strict compliance
for handling “important data” and “personal informa-
tion,” especially when data is transferred across
borders. The 2023 administrative measure, Interim
Measures for the Management of Generative AI
Services, further require that training data come
from “legitimate sources” (Article 7) and that user
inputs be protected (Article 11) (Government of
China, 2023). These rules and regulations create com-
pliance risks for ﬁrms using foreign API services like
ChatGPT, which may transmit user data abroad.
Open-source models, instead, enable local deploy-
ment, allowing ﬁrms to run AI tools on internal
servers. This not only keeps data within national
borders but also ensures greater control over data
provenance, usage, and security, making it a strategic
choice for regulatory compliance.

From a global perspective, China’s open-source AI
push is a deliberate strategy of asymmetric competi-
tion
aimed
at
countering
U.S.
AI
dominance
(OSCHINA, 2024). Lacking the proprietary models,
vast user bases, and advanced semiconductor access
enjoyed by U.S. tech leaders like OpenAI and
Google, China seeks advantage on a different front
by leveraging open-source AI strategy to lower devel-
opment costs, accelerate iteration, and rapidly build a
domestic
developer
community.
This
approach
enables China to sidestep direct competition on expen-
sive infrastructure and proprietary platforms, instead
undermining U.S. advantages by promoting high dif-
fusion at low cost. This strategy shifts operational
costs to proprietary vendors, allowing China to scale
AI capabilities with minimal investment (OSCHINA,
2024). Open-source thus serves as a strategic counter-
measure to erode U.S. dominance by exploiting asym-
metries in innovation and resource allocation.
To achieve this, China has framed its open-source
AI strategy through a narrative of “AI democratiza-
tion” (AI 平民化), positioning itself not as a techno-
logical hegemon but as a Global South partner (Yu,
2025; Zheng, 2025). This strategy reﬂects a contem-
porary form of soft power, understood as the ability
to inﬂuence through attraction rather than coercion
(Nye, 2012). By offering high-performance models
at low cost, China cultivates attraction, particularly
among developing countries which are unable to
afford expensive Western APIs. This narrative of
technological inclusivity is being rolled out through
state media and diplomatic initiatives like the SCO
AI Cooperation Center (involving China, Russia,
and other SCO members) (Xinhua, 2025) and the
China-BRICS AI Development and Cooperation
Center (with Brazil, Russia, India, and South Africa)
(Wang, 2025). These initiatives aim to build coalitions
and position Chinese technology as a credible
non-Western standard for digital development.
This approach also enables China to shape global
AI standards. When governments and developers in
the Global South adopt Chinese open-source AI
models, they are embedding a technical architecture
that creates path dependencies and aligns them with
China’s standards (Chan et al., 2025). Companies
such as Indonesia’s Ruangguru, Russia’s Yandex,
and India’s Yotta have already localized engines
based on Chinese models (Yu, 2025). This collabora-
tive and scalable approach mirrors Android′s global
spread through third-party developers (Yik, 2025).
In the process, China’s vision of digital governance
is diffused. While not explicitly exporting authoritar-
ianism, these models provide a convenient techno-
logical template for digital control, born from an
ecosystem that prioritizes data localization and
content ﬁltering. This makes open-source, as one
tech investor puts it, a potent form of “technological
soft power … the equivalent of Hollywood movies
or the Big Mac in the tech world” (Meaghan, 2025).
China’s open-source AI strategy embodies a
central paradox: it adopts the language of global
openness and democratization while reinforcing a
domestic system of strict political control. This
represents an evolution in digital authoritarianism,
shifting from a defensive posture of internal
control to a proactive global effort to set inter-
national standards. The critical question remains:
can a system rooted in domestic surveillance and
censorship truly earn global trust and become the
basis for a new international standard of openness?.
