AFRICA, AI & CRITICAL MINERALS:

Reclaiming Value at the Source through Ethical Global Governance

By Ifeanyi Aniagoh, MBCSUniversity of Hertfordshire | Artificial Intelligence

PREFACE

This paper was inspired by Nigeria’s position at the 2025 G20 Leaders’ Summit, where the country advocated for ethical global standards in Artificial Intelligence and fair value creation at the source of critical minerals. As a Nigerian researcher studying AI in Business in the United Kingdom, I was personally compelled by this intervention - not merely as policy rhetoric, but as a defining moment in Africa’s digital destiny.

Africa is at the centre of two global transformations: the green economy and the AI economy (UNECA, 2023). Our cobalt powers batteries. Our lithium powers electric vehicles. Our manganese supports semiconductor manufacturing. Yet across history, the presence of resources has rarely translated to prosperity (Mazzucato, 2018). And today, the same imbalance threatens to reappear in digital form, as Africa becomes a contributor of raw data - linguistic patterns, cultural expressions, behavioural insights - without owning the resulting models, algorithms, and intellectual property (O’Neil, 2016).

This paper is therefore both academic and personal. It reflects my lived experience navigating digital systems in the UK as an African migrant, and my long-standing mission as the Digi-Priest to advocate for technology that drives inclusive growth rather than deepening global inequalities.

ABSTRACT

Critical minerals and artificial intelligence now define the geopolitical economy. Africa holds a major share of global mineral deposits and a rapidly expanding digital ecosystem (UNECA, 2023; World Bank, 2022) yet continues to operate at the lowest levels of value creation. This paper argues that Africa risks repeating colonial extractive patterns - exporting minerals and data while importing finished technologies - unless strategic interventions prioritise local beneficiation, sovereign data governance, and ethical AI standards.

Using Nigeria’s G20 stance as a contemporary entry point, the paper examines historical extraction, digital colonialism, structural bias in AI systems, and Africa’s limited participation in global tech governance. It concludes that true digital prosperity requires shifting Africa from resource supplier to value-creation hub through policy reform, industrialisation, inclusive datasets, and AI built on African cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic realities.

1. INTRODUCTION - AFRICA AT A HISTORICAL INFLECTION POINT

Africa is not new to being essential to global progress. Our cotton fuelled European textile mills. Our rubber powered industrial manufacturing. Our oil and gas sustained global energy markets. But at each historical moment, Africa supplied the foundation while others harvested the value.

Today, two forces define the new world order:

Critical Minerals → batteries, chips, renewable infrastructure

Artificial Intelligence → automation, decision-making, digital value creation

Africa sits at the centre of both.

The continent contains:

30% of global mineral reserves

70% of the world’s cobalt

The youngest workforce in human history

The fastest-growing digital adoption rates

Yet being essential is not the same as being empowered.

Historically, Africa exported cocoa and imported chocolate; exported crude oil and imported petrol; exported labour and imported unemployment. Now, Africa exports digital participation and risks importing algorithmic inequality.

AI models trained predominantly on Western data often misinterpret African speech patterns, names, medical features, cultural behaviour, and socioeconomic identity (Ada Lovelace Institute, 2021). Thus, the continent risks not just being exploited materially, but misrepresented digitally.

The question is no longer whether Africa will participate in the AI future - it already is. The real question is:

Will Africa shape AI, or will AI shape Africa?

2. FROM COLONIAL EXTRACTION TO DIGITAL EXTRACTIVISM

The nature of exploitation evolves, but the structure remains familiar (O’Neil, 2016):

Industrial Era Extraction

Digital Era Extraction

Minerals, agriculture, labour

Data, culture, language, behaviour

Ships & railways

Cloud platforms & APIs

Colonial governments

Tech monopolies

Export raw inputs

Export raw datasets

Import finished goods

Import trained models

Africa is no longer only mined through soil; it is now mined through smartphones.

Every tweet, fintech transaction, biometric record, and search query contributes data that trains global AI systems - but data storage, compute, and monetisation occur offshore.

Consequences include:

Weak algorithmic accuracy in African contexts

Foreign ownership of African insights

Cultural misrepresentation in global AI tools

Limited economic capture from digital activity

Like cocoa beans that generate trillion-dollar chocolate industries elsewhere, African data drives value far from where it is produced.

Unless governance changes, digital colonialism will replace physical colonialism.

3. VALUE ADDITION AT SOURCE: FROM SUPPLY TO SOVEREIGNTY

Africa must break the pattern:

We produce the wealth; others harvest the value.

Local beneficiation is not optional; it is foundational to sovereignty.

Priorities include:

Refining manganese, cobalt, and lithium domestically

Manufacturing EV batteries and chip components in Africa

Establishing mineral pricing power through AfCFTA

Mandating local processing before export (as Indonesia did)

Value is not created by having minerals; value is created by controlling how minerals become technology.

This shift transforms Africa from:

Old Role

New Role

Supplier

Manufacturer

Market

Producer

Consumer

Innovator

Participant

Power broker

Without this shift, Africa will remain materially essential but economically expendable.

4. AI AS A DEVELOPMENT EQUALISER - OR DIVIDER

Artificial Intelligence has the potential to accelerate development in ways Africa has never experienced. It can democratise access to education through personalised learning tools, expand healthcare capacity using machine-assisted diagnostics, and support farmers using predictive crop analytics and supply-chain optimisation.

At its best, AI can:

give learners access to world-class education

support medical diagnostics in rural hospitals

enable financial services without traditional banks

improve government service delivery and transparency

These are not hypotheticals - they are emerging realities across Africa.

However, AI also has the capacity to deepen inequality if adopted without structural safeguards. If Africa imports AI tools without shaping the data, policies, and cultural context behind them, AI becomes a reinforcement of past power imbalances.

Three major risks emerge:

4.1 Automation Without Employment

AI can accelerate productivity while eliminating roles that have no alternative employment pathways, particularly in informal economies where safety nets are weak.

4.2 Development Without Representation

AI systems trained on foreign datasets routinely misclassify African contexts, leading to inaccurate results in healthcare, education, policing, and recruitment.

4.3 Innovation Without Ownership

If Africa only consumes AI systems built elsewhere, the continent becomes a customer, not a co-author of global intelligence.

The danger is not AI taking African jobs - it is AI taking African agency.

To truly serve Africa, AI must be built on African data, governed by African laws, and aligned with African priorities.

5. ETHICAL AI GOVERNANCE FOR AFRICA

AI ethics is not merely about fairness; it is about power.

Most global AI standards are being drafted in:

Brussels

Washington

Beijing

Silicon Valley

Meanwhile, Africa - one of the largest contributors of data and minerals - is largely absent from the negotiation table.

If Africa does not participate in shaping global AI rules, the continent will be shaped by them.

5.1 Principles Africa Must Defend

Principle

Imperative

Transparency

Citizens must know data being collected & how decisions are made

Equity

AI must not reinforce racial or economic hierarchies

Localization

Data should be stored & processed under African jurisdiction

Beneficiation

Economic value must return to communities generating data

This is not regulation as restriction - it is regulation as liberation.

5.2 Institutional Action Required

Africa needs:

Continental AI policy under the African Union

National AI strategies with legislative backing

African-owned cloud & computer infrastructure

Investment in African NLP, datasets & open-source models

Governance must evolve from compliance to capability.

6. FROM RAW MATERIALS TO RAW DATA: A NEW FRONTIER OF EXPLOITATION

Data is becoming to AI what crude oil was to industrial power.

If Africa does not secure data sovereignty:

Models will learn from African users without benefitting them

Cultural knowledge will be commodified without attribution

African languages will be digitised without authorship

Local innovation will be dependent on foreign APIs and platforms

This is how extraction evolves quietly.

AI does not just take what Africa produces - it takes what Africa knows.

This new exploitation is subtle, automated, and decentralised. It occurs not at mines or borders, but in databases and servers.

The remedy is simple but ambitious:

Recognise data as a national asset

Require value return for training AI models

Mandate local data storage and processing

Create African data trusts and sovereign registries

Minerals built the industrial world. Data will build the AI world.

Africa must own both.

7. POLICY PATHWAYS FOR AFRICA’S DIGITAL SOVEREIGNTY

Africa’s goal must shift from participation to power.

The pathway requires transformation across five pillars:

7.1 Continental Governance

Harmonise policies under AfCFTA

Create joint negotiation power for minerals & data

Align with global standards on African terms

7.2 Local Industrialisation

Ban raw exports of strategic minerals

Provide incentives for battery & chip manufacturing

Build refinery corridors across regional blocs

7.3 Talent & Knowledge Systems

Integrate AI into STEM curricula

Build AI research hubs in universities

Train local model builders, not just users

7.4 Infrastructure

African-owned cloud and compute resources

Data residency laws

Open access to public sector datasets

7.5 Public–Private Collaboration

Research partnerships with industry

Innovation sandboxes

Venture & grants for African AI companies

8. AFRICA’S MIGRANT EXPERIENCE AS EVIDENCE OF BIAS

The debate on AI fairness often feels abstract until it becomes personal.As an African migrant living and working in the United Kingdom, I have repeatedly experienced exclusion from AI-driven systems not because of lack of competence, but because the data they were trained on simply did not recognise me.

This appears in real contexts:

Recruitment platforms filtering out candidates with non-Western names

Credit scoring systems marking migrants as “high risk” due to thin digital footprints

Voice recognition tools misinterpreting African accents

Automated application portals rejecting foreign qualifications

Healthcare algorithms misreading biological markers not trained on Black physiology

These are not glitches - they are symptoms of datasets that did not include us.

AI does not simply exclude individuals; it quietly writes entire communities out of the digital future.

When the future is built on data that does not see you, that future does not belong to you.

This is why Africa must not only adopt AI - it must also author AI.

9. A NEW MODEL FOR VALUE-SHARING IN THE AI ECONOMY

Africa must shift from being a participant in global AI to being a co-owner of value.

This requires a new governance philosophy:

Old Mindset

New Model

Africa supplies raw materials

Africa supplies and processes raw materials

Data flows outward

Data flows with shared control & compensation

Africa consumes AI

Africa builds & exports AI

Value captured offshore

Value shared at source

This transformation is not charity - it is economics.

To unlock digital prosperity, Africa must:

Own the minerals powering global tech

Own the data training global models

Own the institutions defining global ethics

Africa has contributed too much to be treated as an afterthought.

CONCLUSION - BUILDING VALUE WHERE VALUE ORIGINATES

Africa stands at a crossroads familiar yet historic.

We have the minerals that power batteries, and the data that powers intelligence. But history shows that being essential to global progress does not guarantee participation in global prosperity.

The opportunity ahead is profound:

Africa can become not just a supplier of resources, but a creator of value; not just a participant in AI, but a co-author of intelligence; not just a market, but a force.

This demands courage, industrialisation, intentional policy, and continental unity.But above all, it demands a refusal to repeat the past.

Africa must ensure that the next era of human progress is not built on African inputs and foreign outputs.

The minerals are ours.

The data is ours.

The future must be ours too.
