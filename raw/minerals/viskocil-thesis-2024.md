Abstract
This paper considers the competition between the U.S. and the People’s Republic
of China (PRC). Recent years have seen the development of a loose “China consensus” in
Washington. This analysis takes this new paradigm as a baseline and seeks to add a
contribution to academic and policy discussions through three case studies. The
intersection of critical technologies and global supply chains is the focus of the analysis,
and support is rendered for the general thesis of derisking. Chapter 1 explores China’s
dominance in critical minerals via two historical case studies: the strategies employed by
the War Production Board (WPD) in World War II to mitigate critical material shortages
as well as the oil crises of the 1970s. Chapter 2 explores the AI competition between the
U.S. and PRC by analyzing China’s AI progress as well as reviewing strategy proposals
from policy experts and concludes with several recommendations for policymakers.
Chapter 3 discusses U.S.-PRC competition in Latin American and the Caribbean, setting
forth the view that the region offers a number of potential economic opportunities to both
counter PRC influence as well as derisk critical supply chains. The paper concludes by
proffering three overall conclusions: (1) so-called weaponized interdependence now
characterizes the relationship between the U.S. and PRC, and policymakers must act
accordingly; (2) protective economic policies will be required over the course of the U.S.-
PRC competition, but they should be targeted and implemented in concert with allies
whenever possible; (3) policymakers must be prepared to iteratively revise and update rules
and procedures as necessary.

Introduction

The New Washington Consensus

This paper represents an attempt to provide a contribution to a highly complicated
matter of policy and academic study, namely, the competition between the United States
and the People’s Republic of China (PRC). Few subjects have prompted as much
discussion in Washington in recent years as the nature of this contest. The past decade
has witnessed the formation of a rough bipartisan consensus around the need for a
tougher China policy. Intellectual property theft, violations of trade rules, naval
aggression in the South China Sea, and murky handling of the COVID-19 pandemic have
evidenced the PRC’s aggressive global designs. For many in Washington, this has led to
a call for a more aggressive U.S. response.1 As the 2022 National Security Strategy
stated, “The PRC … is the only competitor with both the intent to reshape the
international order and, increasingly, the economic, diplomatic, military, and
technological power to advance that objective.”2 In the view of much of the national
security establishment, the Pax Americana is dead, and a new era of multipolar

Research Methodology & Structure
This paper takes Washington’s newfound China hawkery as a baseline. While
there is certainly justification for ongoing debate regarding such a presupposition, it is not
the scope of this analysis. The author candidly situates this study within the vein of
commentators and policymakers who view the PRC’s ascendence as a fundamental
challenge to U.S. interests and suggest more aggressive action. This should not be
interpreted as an embrace of groupthink or a denial of the need for more skeptical
analyses. Instead, the emphasis of the paper is to provide a relevant contribution to
current policy deliberation and formulation.
One could not, and should not, attempt to address the entirety of the U.S. – PRC
competition in the space provided by this master’s thesis. Instead, it trains its focus on
two highly enmeshed themes: critical technology and supply chain derisking. As U.S.-
China tensions have elevated in recent years, technology – from Huawei to TikTok to AI
– has been at the center. This has only intensified in the wake of the COVID-19
pandemic, as many countries have become wary of overreliance on the PRC and have
sought to enhance supply chain security. At present, the most in vogue formulation is
derisking, which is essentially gradual and targeted decoupling from the PRC in critical

Key Conclusions

At a strategic level, this thesis supports three conclusions. First, what some
scholars have called “weaponized interdependence” is fast becoming, or has perhaps
already become, standard operating procedure for many on the global stage.21 The PRC
has used its critical mineral dominance for diplomatic leverage, and the U.S. has
leveraged its tech superiority to impose sweeping export controls on Beijing. U.S.
policymakers should continue to consider ways in which the nation’s economic strengths
can be effectively harnessed in the competition with China.

Second, protective economic policies may be required. However, these should be
minimally relied upon and targeted to the most strategic sectors. Industrial policies can
lead to cronyism and economic inefficiency, which could portend longer term challenges
for U.S. industry. Policymakers should work to enhance the competitive capabilities of
U.S. firms. Moreover, partnerships with key allies will prove crucial. From agreements
on critical minerals to collective action on semiconductor export controls, U.S. policy
will not succeed in a vacuum.
Finally, the dynamics of the U.S.-PRC are continually evolving. While U.S. grand
strategy should remain constant, operational tactics cannot remain stagnant. Export

Political Considerations

This paper was prepared during and shortly after the 2024 U.S. presidential
election. Given the uncertainty of the result for much of the analyses, this thesis seeks to
discuss longer-term trends wherever possible. Of course, one cannot fully separate such a
study from politics. The incoming Trump administration will certainly bring about a
number of different policy priorities and implementation tactics. The President-elect has
already promised an additional tariff of ten percent on all Chinese imports.22 Whether or
not this aggressive trade rhetoric will correspond to action, or a desire to escalate in other
areas of the competition, remains to be seen.

the topics of Chapters 1 and 2 – critical minerals and artificial intelligence, at least in
regard to international politics. Both Republicans and Democrats have expressed alarm at
U.S. import dependency on China for critical minerals, though the parties have
differences on the solution. Moreover, artificial intelligence is recognized across the aisle
as one of the most important aspects of the competition between the U.S. and PRC. Many
of the administration’s export controls have been widely supported by Republicans and

Introduction
Throughout history, raw materials have been an essential component of state
development and conflict. Ancient groups shaped empires through bronze and iron. In the
nineteenth and twentieth centuries, power and progress were driven by steel, coal, and
oil. The twenty-first century is no different. The development of smartphones, data
centers, electric vehicles, and artificial intelligence is reliant upon critical materials such
as lithium, cobalt, and silicon.24 Continuous invention and competition in these sectors
will only continue to have immense ramifications for the geopolitical landscape – a
development Eric Schmidt, former Google CEO, has dubbed “innovation power.”25
In the United States, discussions among policymakers and commentators
regarding raw materials have grown more urgent in recent years. The COVID-19
pandemic exposed supply chain vulnerabilities and has prompted a slew of policy

Case Study Selection Criteria
Resource-driven, geopolitical tension is nothing new; history offers many
poignant examples.59 The three case studies that form the core of this analysis have been
selected for several reasons. Three methodological considerations factored prominently in
their determination. First, these cases represent instances in which the U.S. faced acute,
measurable raw materials shortages. Such shortages are an inherent reality of economies
and supply chains. However, the events of the Second World War and the oil crises in the
1970s were prolonged and commanded significant public and private attention. Copious
information exists about both cases, allowing for deeper analysis. Furthermore, as the
focus of this chapter is ultimately the strategic considerations relevant to current
questions regarding critical materials, relevant historical case studies must observe events
that demand the attention of high-level national policymakers.

Case Study 2: 1970s Oil Shocks

In the fall of 1973, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries
(OAPEC) sharply curtailed oil production and placed an embargo on the United States
for supporting Israel in the Yom Kippur War against Egypt and Syria. The ensuing
energy crisis in the West produced a number of policy frameworks still in effect today.
For the purposes of this chapter, the oil shocks of the 1970s provide an essential case
study in strategy and policy formation in response to an acute and highly significant raw
materials shortage.
Prelude: U.S. Oil in the Early Cold War

Oil has long been an essential component of national and international politics. As
such, the U.S. oil industry has had close ties to the government. To gain an understanding
of the multiplicity of factors that combined to produce the 1973 Arab Oil Embargo, one

Case Study 2 Analysis
The oil shocks of the 1970s (indeed, the enduring geopolitical significance of oil
overall) present a highly beneficial case study. The reason is quite obvious. Just as OPEC
nations were capable of using their oil exports as a political tool in the 1970s, much to the
shock of the U.S., the PRC now possesses similar power given its near monopoly on the
processing and refining of critical materials (and rare earths in particular). Sophia
Kalantzakos has produced a detailed comparison of oil and strategic minerals. Her
assessment is worth quoting at some length:
Although rare earths in their natural forms, like oil, appear to be geologically
abundant, they remain scarce as a mineable resource, and their strategic
importance as a commodity has not diminished because, as we have seen,
substitution has proven difficult, in effect, testing the limits of the notion that
innovation is a panacea for scarcity. Moreover, just as with oil, new mining
projects outside China that had seemed very promising when the prices of the
elements had skyrocketed are no longer the alternatives the world thought they
were because they are no longer economically viable.117

Building upon Kalantzakos’ evaluation, three considerations can be drawn from this case
study.

First, when faced with resource scarcity in the context of modern geopolitics, the
instruments of the state are limited. Indeed, both of the case studies observed demonstrate
that governments essentially have four overall strategies from which to choose: (1)
innovation and ultimate substitution for the resources in question; (2) government
involvement in the allocation of resources; (3) diversification of resource supply; (4)
stockpiling of resources. The first option, while certainly desirable, has not yet been able
to produce the intended result (i.e., a significant reduction in supply dependencies). As

Conclusion

Critical materials are likely to be a subject of political concern for some time.
Without a significant change in the bilateral relationship between the U.S. and the PRC,
both countries seem likely to continue to view certain sectors of the mining industry in an
ever more political light. Such a prediction is underscored by Washington’s pivot to
various forms of industrial policy (though the shift is certainly not universally
endorsed).127 Against this political backdrop, an examination of what relevant
considerations historical case studies of raw material issues can provide seems quite
reasonable.
Several considerations have been proffered in this analysis. The successes and
challenges of the Controlled Materials Plan during the Second World War demonstrate
the essential nature of sound data and the need for bureaucratic structures to emphasize
program modifications given lessons learned. Perhaps more noteworthy, however, is the
need for policymakers to factor downstream effects into their planning processes. This
will be a particularly poignant application as much of the concern over critical materials
import dependencies is generated within the civilian economy. Even if U.S. mining
companies can overcome domestic challenges and begin significant stateside operations,
demand is likely to naturally flow to cheaper foreign options. Thus, it is not unreasonable

Analysis & Recommendations

For good or ill, artificial intelligence is fast becoming a core element of state
power. Its military applications are already underway, and it would appear to portend
substantial economic changes. AI is bound to exacerbate geopolitical tensions between
the U.S. and PRC. This is mainly due to preexistent geostrategic factors. However, the

In the West, countries—with the U.S. leading—are largely focused on the dangers

contrast, China is almost exclusively focused on research and development
(R&D) and is devoting very little energy to constraining possible negative
outcomes from AI development.216

progress hitherto, should prompt concern among policymakers.

U.S. Policy in LAC: The Monroe Doctrine & Economic Engagement
Before discussing proposed strategies to counter PRC advances in LAC, it is
essential to begin with historical context. U.S. foreign policy in Latin America has often
been understood within the heritage of the Monroe Doctrine. An appreciation of its
enduring significance is a useful starting point. First stated by James Monroe in 1823 in
order to deter European powers from meddling in the Western hemisphere, the doctrine
maintained:
…as a principle in which the rights and interests of the United States are involved,
that the American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have
assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future
colonization by any European powers. … We owe it, therefore, to candor and to the
amicable relations existing between the United States and those powers to declare
that we should consider any attempt on their part to extend their system to any
portion of this hemisphere as dangerous to our peace and safety.255
In his declaration, President Monroe established two essential pillars of American foreign
policy with regard to the Western hemisphere: (1) “non-colonization” and (2) “non-
interference.”256 The policy formalized foundational elements of American political

An Academic Paradigm for Further Analysis: Regionalism
In addition to policy-focused literature, generally aimed at the practitioner
community, it is useful to consider academic paradigms in analyzing the PRC’s role in
LAC. A substantial portion of the available literature focuses on economic integration.
Often discussed within the paradigm of regionalism, this provides highly beneficial
insights for research and policy formation.
Perhaps one of the more poorly clarified concepts in the field of International
Relations, regionalism, at the risk of oversimplification, seeks to study the economic and
political interconnectivity between states within a “region” – a term that is somewhat
opaque. Rick Fawn summed up the nebulous concept rather well: “While not ideal,
historians and political scientists are said to 'know a region when they see one', and
economists identify them through the existence of formal trading structures.”300 Fawn’s
quip is both humorous and informative. While one certainly cannot find any kind of
standard definition, many scholars seem to include many of the same characteristics in
their discussion of a region (e.g., geography, cultural identity, institutions, etc.).301

Generally, regionalism is bifurcated into “new” and “old” approaches. Old
regionalism is often associated with post-World War II European efforts at economic
integration (generally 1950-1970), which produced organizational structures such as the
European Coal and Steel Community in the 1950s, and eventually the European Union.
Interstate peace was the primary catalyst for the development of these institutions: “Both
policy-makers and scholars usually viewed the (Westphalian) nation-state as the problem
rather than the solution, and the purpose of ‘regional integration’ was to avoid war.”302
While much of the early academic literature on the subject was Eurocentric, developing
nations pursued regionalist schemes as well, though the impetus for cooperation here was
less a desire to avoid the catastrophes of war and instead a desire to foster greater
economic development.303 “New” regionalism (typically classified as spanning much of
the 1980s and 90s) pursued a different, more open form of cooperation. Whereas prior
decades had seen cooperation in concert with protectionism and an emphasis on security,
the end of the Cold War ushered in an era of economic neoliberalism and the increased
international engagement of non-state economic actors (e.g., executives of multinational
companies).304 This is sometimes referred to as “open” regionalism.305

Given the discussion hitherto, it does not seem unreasonable to suggest that U.S.
policymakers must adopt a more substantial hemispheric and LAC strategy. A brief
survey of the publicly available data demonstrates that the PRC’s presence in Latin

China’s approach as one of “economic displacement.”316 Of course, the empirical reality

officials. As Berg, Brands, Ellis, and others have argued, a new and focused strategy is
needed that recommits the U.S. to the region, emphasizes the need for additional Western
backed funding, attempts to offer LAC allies competitive economic counteroffers, and
reforms relevant government bureaucracies when needed.

Conclusion

The People’s Republic of China represents a significant challenge to U.S. global
power and influence. Such is the consensus of the foreign policy establishment, and it is
not unjustified. While other conflicts rage around the world and necessarily demand the
attention of U.S. policymakers, this reality must remain paramount. As the foreign policy
scholar Walter Russel Mead has recently written, “Wars in the Middle East and Ukraine
dominate the headlines, but the Indo-Pacific remains the fulcrum of world politics and
where the 21st century will take shape.”331

Nowhere is this reality more poignant than the critical technologies and supply
chains that deliver them to nations, multinational corporations, and individuals. The
COVID-19 pandemic exposed a number of vulnerabilities in the current supply chain
system, and ultimately led Congress to enact several significant pieces of legislation (e.g.,
the Chips Act and the Inflation Reduction Act) aimed at reviving essential sectors of the
U.S. economy. However, as any good politician will reflexively clarify, there is still more
to be done.

This paper sought to bring a contribution to this discussion about what more can
and should be done. It advanced this goal through three chapters, each dealing with U.S.-
PRC competition and each dealing in some way with the enmeshed supply lines and
technological landscapes of the two nations. Chapter 1 explored critical minerals, the
essential building blocks of most modern technologies. China presents an enormous

Key Conclusions

As stated at the outset, this thesis has supported three conclusions: (1) weaponized
interdependence is a new norm of strategic competition; (2) some protective economic
policies will be necessary; (3) policy formation should be an iterative task, given the fluid
and likely protracted nature of the U.S.-PRC competition.

leveraging economic strengths to accomplish aggressive, hostile geopolitical goals – has
become standard operating procedure in this new era of great power competition.335 While
some might attribute more of this weaponization process to the U.S., the PRC has long
engaged in such behaviors as well.336 Often, China has gone further, engaging in blatantly
extortive or illegal behavior. For example, a 2023 report by the House Select Committee
on the Communist Party of China suggested that the PRC stole “as much as $600 billion
per year of intellectual property (IP) and technology…”337 However, as this paper has
shown, China has also used legal methods of economic belligerence. It used critical

Policy Formation Must be Iterative
Finally, policymakers must be prepared to act in an iterative manner, revising
policies and procedures as necessary. The competition between the U.S. and PRC is likely
to be long. While the U.S. should pursue an overall strategy of victory, as former China
Committee Chairman Mike Gallagher has argued, operational tactics must be subject to
constant analysis, and as needed, revision.352 Export controls lists must be modified. As
nearshoring and friendshoring activities occur, mineral criticality will certainly need to be
evaluated and revised. U.S. officials should consider ways to streamline policy review and
implementation timelines to allow for more rapid responses when needed.
It is likely that such efficiency reforms would be an effort originating in the
executive branch, as it is charged with executing the nation’s foreign policy. Many of the
sanctions wielded against the PRC, for example, are developed by the Treasury and
Commerce Departments. Efforts aimed at streamlining the development and deployment
of such policy instruments would thus naturally originate within the departments and
agencies that have jurisdiction. However, lawmakers would also do well to consider ways
in which they might be able to make U.S. foreign policy formation more efficient.

consensus regarding China, it is quite broad. There is much room for fluctuation within

Further Research Opportunities

While the majority of this paper has emphasized ways in which policymakers
might pragmatically respond to challenges from the PRC, the subject of U.S.-China
competition also offers substantial opportunities for further academic research. While
senior government officials likely do not consult academic work in their decision-making
processes, there are still important linkages between the academy and policy
communities. Further research will thus contribute (though perhaps in the longer term) to
policy enhancement.

Three areas stand out as ripe for additional inquiry. First, the nature of derisking
deserves much more serious and substantial consideration. While the term itself is a
product of political rhetoric, the concept of targeted decoupling, in concert with allies, to
mitigate economic and strategic risks is a serious idea that has gained acceptance among
many top Western government officials. In many ways, one could view derisking as a

The Path Forward
The challenges posed by the People’s Republic of China are enormous. The U.S.
faces no other challenger with the resources and intentions to overtake its power and
influence on the world stage. From raw materials to artificial intelligence, to security of the
nation in the Western hemisphere, the PRC presents the most substantial challenge to U.S.
global power in a generation. However, the magnitude of the contest should not be cause
for pessimism. U.S. success is far from guaranteed, but it possesses numerous strengths:
abundant raw material resources; one of the most innovative economies in the world, able
to design products such as advanced semiconductors; and a hemisphere that portends
significant political and economic opportunities. Leveraging the interplay between all three
will prove critical in winning the competition with the PRC.
