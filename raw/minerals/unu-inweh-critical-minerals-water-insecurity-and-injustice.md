The global energy, digital, and
sustainability transitions have significantly
increased the demand for critical minerals,
which are essential for the development
and operation of artificial intelligence
(AI), renewable energy technologies,
and clean transportation systems.
Projections suggest that this demand will
quadruple by 2050 if current consumption
patterns and extractive policies remain
unchanged. Specifically, the demand
for lithium, graphite, and cobalt could
increase by approximately 500% by 2050.
This prevailing extractive trajectory is
marked by serious environmental and
social injustices that are often overlooked
or obscured by dominant economic,
political and even sustainability transition
narratives.
This report by the United Nations
University Institute for Water, Environment
and Health (UNU-INWEH) on its 30th
anniversary presents a comprehensive
analysis of the fundamental contradictions
at the heart of this global transition with
a special focus on the water sector. The
report discusses how the accelerated
pursuit of critical minerals is directly
contributing to acute water insecurity,
livelihood disruptions, and health risks
in many of the world’s most vulnerable
regions. It further highlights how
disparities in environmental and health
outcomes expose structural inequalities,
revealing who benefits from, and who bears
the costs of, the global energy, digital,
and sustainability transitions. Leveraging
empirical anlyses, scientific studies, policy
documents, and media reports, this report
synthesizes evidence on the extraction of
critical minerals from both Global North
and South, specifically drawing on data
from the Lithium Triangle, the Democratic
Republic of the Congo (DRC), and other
high-risk regions.
The extraction of critical minerals
significantly exacerbates water insecurity,
primarily through excessive freshwater
use and the contamination of local water
sources. This not only threatens access
to safe water for nearby communities but
also undermines agricultural livelihoods
and food production, particularly in
regions already facing water scarcity.
Mining operations frequently discharge
effluents containing toxic chemicals into
rivers and streams, degrading water quality
and reducing availability. Such pollution
directly violates the fundamental human
right to safe water and disrupts water-
dependent livelihoods, including farming,
fishing, and livestock rearing, further
limiting household access to clean drinking
water.
The health risks associated with
mining-related water contamination are
especially severe in communities located
near extraction sites. Evidence from
regions such as South Africa’s Limpopo
Province, Bolivia’s Uyuni region and the
DRC reveals widespread heavy metal
contamination and toxic waste exposure.
These environmental hazards have led
to serious health problems, particularly
among women and children, including
reproductive harm, birth defects, and
chronic illnesses.

The report argues that the extractive
industry for critical minerals is marked
by deep structural injustices and global
inequalities. Most extraction occurs in the
Global South, where local communities
disproportionately bear the environmental
degradation, and livelihood disruptions
associated with mining activities. In
contrast, the economic, technological,
environmental, and health benefits of
these minerals largely accrue to wealthy
nations, communities and individuals,
mostly in the Global North. This imbalance
raises a pressing question: Does the global
race for critical minerals represent a new
form of extractive colonialism, replicating
the injustices of the fossil fuel era under
the banner of sustainability?
The evidence from this investigation
suggests that without deliberate policy
intervention, the current trajectory of
mineral extraction risks entrenching
existing inequalities and further harming
vulnerable communities. This report
therefore underscores the urgent need
to protect water resources from the
harmful impacts of mineral extraction.
Governments must adopt and uphold
robust environmental policies, including
strict enforcement of environmental laws,
regular public testing for pollutants,
rehabilitation of degraded lands and
ecosystems, and integration of Indigenous
and local knowledge into environmental
management to promote sustainable
extraction and ecosystem restoration.
Effective implementation will depend
on institutional capacity, cross-sector
collaboration, and dedicated funding for
long-term environmental recovery.
To address the hidden trade-offs in critical
mineral supply chains, global governance
systems must be strengthened. This
includes establishing mandatory
international standards for due diligence to
replace voluntary compliance, promoting
North–South policy coordination and
legally binding mechanisms for ethical
sourcing and environmental justice, and
enforcing strict pollution and wastewater
controls, including zero-discharge systems,
requiring independent monitoring of
water use, waste management, and heavy
metal contamination. It is also important
to ensure regular public disclosure
of environmental data to enhance
transparency and accountability.
Additionally, a circular economy
framework is essential to conserve finite
resources and reduce the environmental
and social harms of large-scale mining.
Governments and international partners
should invest in research, innovation,
and technology for material substitution,
waste mitigation, and sustainable
product design. Furthermore, they need
to develop advanced recycling systems
for batteries, electronics, and renewable
energy components, support industries
that repurpose waste materials, and
create incentives for companies to design
products with longer life cycles and greater
recyclability.

14
To meet global climate targets, demand for key critical minerals (e.g., lithium, graphite,
cobalt) is projected to quadruple by 2050.
Global reserves for critical minerals are concentrated in vulnerable regions with about 16%
in high water stress regions and 54% of energy transition minerals on or near indigenous
territories.
The extraction of critical minerals is water-intensive and about 1.9 of million litres of water
is needed to produce just a tonne of lithium in some areas.
In 2024, global lithium output was about 240,000 tons, demanding an estimated 456 billion
liters of water, equivalent to the annual domestic water needs of 62 million people in sub-
Saharan Africa.
For every ton of rare earth minerals produced, about 2,000 tons of toxic waste are
generated.
In 2024, global rare earth production is estimated to have produced about 707 million
metric tons of toxic waste, equivalent to nearly 59 million loaded garbage trucks.
Critical minerals extraction intensifies water insecurity by depleting freshwater sources,
polluting existing supplies, and undermining people's capability to access safe water.
In regions like Chile's Salar de Atacama, lithium and other mining accounts for up to 65% of
regional water usage, intensifying competition with agriculture and domestic needs leading
to dramatic groundwater depletion.
In major critical minerals producing nations like the DRC (Cobalt), over 80% of mineral
output is controlled by foreign industrial mines, limiting local economic gains and value-
addition.
Despite the vast mineral wealth, 73.5% of the population in the DRC lives on less than $2.15
a day, highlighting a stark local economic disparity.
Extraction of critical minerals contributes to water pollution and freshwater depletion,
posing serious health risks disproportionately affecting women and children.
Empirical studies show that 72% of respondents near mining sites in the DRC reported skin
diseases, and 56% of women and girls reported gynecological issues, demonstrating the
disproportionate health burden.
In DRC, neural tube defects, lower limb defects, cleft lip, and abdominal wall defects have
been higher in maternal wards located near mining areas compared to those situated farther
away.
In communities close to DRC's mining areas, neural tube defects are occurring at a rate of
10.9 per 10,000 births, followed by lower limb defects at 8.8 per 10,000 births.
About 30% of mining sites in the DRC employ children, who often lack basic health and
safety protections.
The lack of global governance model for critical minerals undermines progress toward the
sustainable development goals (SDGs).
The social and environmental costs of extracting critical minerals are often borne by
marginalized communities in the Global South, while the technological, economic,
environmental and health benefits primarily accrue to industrialized nations and their
populations.

The world is currently experiencing two
interconnected transitions. The first is a
widespread technological transformation
that involves integrating digital technologies,
such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), clean
energy solutions (including solar panels,
wind turbines, battery storage, hydrogen
electrolyzers, and fuel cells), and electric
vehicles (EVs). The second transition is a shift
toward net-zero economies, often referred to
as the green transition. Both transitions are
linked, as advancements in digital technologies
can pave the way for the green transition by
helping to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.
These transitions along with the continuous
development of modern military infrastructure
and defence systems rely on critical minerals
and have intensified their demand. Critical
minerals, which include cobalt (Co), copper
(Cu), lithium (Li), cadmium (Cd), and some rare
earth elements (REEs), are non-fuel minerals
or metals vital to the functioning of modern
technologies such AI, EVs, solar panels,
and wind turbines, among others.1,2 Critical
minerals are essential for economic and
national security, serving as the oil of the 21st
century, i.e., the fuel of the Fourth Industrial
Revolution (Industry 4.0). They are what make
EV batteries small yet powerful enough for
longer driving ranges; they make it possible
for the development and functionality of AI,
and they are indispensable for maximizing the
performance and durability of smartphones
and laptops. These are just a few of the many
technologies that rely on critical minerals.
The demand for critical minerals tripled
between 2010 and 2023 with lithium,3 for
example, recording a 30% increase in demand
in 2022 alone, while cobalt and nickel demand
grew by 70% and 40%, respectively, from 2017
to 2022.3,4 This trend is expected to continue
with demand for all critical minerals projected
to increase sharply to more than double by
2030 and quadrupled by 2050.4,5 Between 2000
and 2022, the total market size for critical
minerals more than doubled, reaching over
USD 320 billion in trade value.3 Projections
suggest that demand for graphite, lithium,
and cobalt could rise by nearly 500% by 2050,
compared to 2020 levels, if the world is to
meet the carbon budgets of 1·5°C or 2·0°C.6

However, this surge in demand for rare
earth minerals has a major hidden cost.8–13
While critical mineral can be found in many
countries across the world, most of these
minerals are extracted in parts of the Global
South, where extraction often comes at a
great social and environmental expense.8–10,14–19
Mining communities across the globe have
faced environmental degradation, health
problems, and unsafe or exploitative working
conditions.2,19–22
Meanwhile, the benefits of the green and
digital transitions (the advanced technologies
and economic gains) are mostly reaped
elsewhere by wealthier nations and consumers,
mostly in the Global North. This imbalance
between who benefits and who bears the
burdens is raising serious concerns about
whether the world's digital revolution and
transition to sustainability is just and fair. A
problem is thereby posed which has become
augmented in the light of the injustices
associated with the past human experiences
with oil and fossil fuel extraction in developing
countries: Could the global rush to extract
critical minerals be a reproduction of the
neoliberal extractive colonialism which is
worsening pre-existing inequalities and
injustices in the Global South? In other words,
can it be argued that in its rush toward
sustainability, the world is employing a set
of policy and economic tools that facilitate
deregulation, privatization, and extraction
of natural resources, labor, and wealth from
less developed regions for the benefit of more
powerful nations or corporations with the
"sustainability transition" justification?23,24
The extraction of critical minerals contributes
directly to the depletion and contamination
of freshwater sources, thereby jeopardizing
local livelihoods that depend on these
resources and creating considerable public
health risks.25–27 Yet, the extractive operations
of these critical minerals are concentrated
in contexts where about a billion people
still lack access to basic drinking water, a
significant threat to population health and
wellbeing. The anticipated increase in demand
for critical minerals will place additional
pressure on local freshwater resources in
mining regions and exacerbate existing water
struggles among local populations, including
the farming communities who rely on scarce
water resources for their food and income.
This has created a significant, but neglected
trade-off and injustice, where the global
transition to sustainability in the digital age
costs the health and well-being of highly
vulnerable communities and groups in certain
parts of the world. While some aspects of this
injustice along the critical minerals supply
chain have received the attention of academics
and media, the water, health and sanitations
aspects remain overlooked.

Critical minerals have two distinct
characteristics: 1) they are non-fuel
minerals vital to the functioning of modern
technologies, economies, or national security,
and 2) they have supply chains that are
susceptible to disruption.2 Thus, the term
‘critical’ is used to describe not only their
significance for modern technologies but also
their rising demand and vulnerability to supply
disruptions.
A mineral's criticality is determined by
prevailing geopolitical and market conditions
at a given time and is subject to change.4
Hence, the criticality of a mineral varies
depending on the purpose, the time scale
and context. As such, several regions and
countries in the world classify their ‘critical’
minerals based on perceived state interests
of geopolitical, economic and technological
parameters.
For example, the United States of America
(USA), under Presidential Executive Order
13817 of 20 December 2017, classify critical
minerals based on three parameters: 1)
minerals vital to economic and national
security; 2) susceptible to supply chain
disruptions; and 3) vital in product
manufacturing.2 Based on these parameters,
the US Geological Survey (USGS) delineated
50 critical minerals in 2022,38 23 of which
have been evaluated for their criticality in
clean energy,39 and 45 are also considered
critical minerals of interest by the US Defense
Logistics Agency (USDLA).
The European Union (EU) has identified
34 critical raw materials considering their
economic importance and supply risk to EU
countries, and 17 of these are delineated
as strategic raw materials due to their
significance for national security, technology
and digital transition.40 Strategic minerals
differ from critical minerals in that their supply
chains are not vulnerable. Australia’s critical
mineral list for 2024 which replaced the 2022
edition, contains 31 minerals.41,42 This list added
nickel to Australia’s critical minerals which was
previously classified as a strategic material.
The Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development (OECD) considers 12 to 20
minerals critical for its member countries,43 a
narrower range than the International Energy
Agency (IEA), which lists 26 critical minerals.42
Each country or region delineates their list of
minerals based on some specific indicators
and priorities. Thus, the ‘criticality’ of a
mineral is a subjective construct shaped
by the strategic considerations of national
interests. For example, the US, Europe,
and Australia classified certain groups of
minerals as ‘critical’ because they found them
essential to national security and economic
transition while  being susceptible to supply
risk.39,41 However, the same minerals may not
be 'critical' for some countries in the Global
South, particularly in Africa, as most of these
countries are less industrialized and lack the
technology to process these minerals for
national benefit,4 sustainability transition, and
national security. This suggests that although
there is a race for critical minerals in Africa
and other regions in the Global South, these
resources primarily serve the geopolitical
and economic interests of the Global North,

3.1 Water Insecurity
3.1.1 Water Scarcity and Depletion
Extracting critical mineral requires a
substantial amount of water, especially during
the mining and processing stages.2,4,68 This
poses a serious threat to freshwater supplies,
which are already scarce in many mining areas,
making local communities more vulnerable.
Lithium extraction, for instance, is extremely
water-intensive,69–71 with estimates indicating
that about 1.9 million liters of water is needed
to produce just one ton of lithium.68,72  An
average lithium mine producing 11,000 tons
of lithium annually73,74 uses roughly 20 billion
liters of water. This amount is enough to cover
the annual domestic water needs of 2.8 million
people in sub-Saharan Africa.
In 2024, US production excluded, the global
lithium production was about 240,000 metric
tons.73 Processing this volume of lithium
would require about 456 billion litres of water,
sufficient to meet the annual domestic water
need of about 62 million people in sub-Saharan
Africa. In lithium-rich regions such as Chile’s
Salar de Atacama (where freshwater resources
are already scarce) lithium and copper mining
is estimated to account for up to 65% of
regional water usage, surpassing the combined
demand from key sectors like agriculture
and industry.72 Globally, approximately 16%
of critical mineral mining sites are located in
areas already classified as water-stressed.72,75
Expanding extraction in these regions is
therefore likely to exacerbate water scarcity,
intensify competition for limited freshwater
supplies, and threaten the availability of
water for other equally critical sectors
including agriculture, domestic consumption,
and industry.76,77  Severe water scarcity also
disrupts both the ecological and hydrological
cycles in the long term and impact water
resource availability. These impacts are
already evident in mining regions of Bolivia and
Chile.18,68,78
The excessive usage of water by the mining
industry contributes to the depletion of
groundwater79,80 and that exacerbates water
insecurity risks in communities that are
already facing water scarcity. The Lithium
Triangle illustrates this challenge vividly.
The triangle, encompassed by the borders
of Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile is one of the
world's richest places in terms of lithium
reserves while being one of the world's poorest
places in terms of water availability.68,72,77 In
such arid environments, lithium mining is
projected to further escalate the problem of
water scarcity to unprecedented levels,80,81
increasing both ecological degradation and
the vulnerability of local populations.65,72,76,82

3.3 Economic, Environmental, Social and
Health Injustices
The impacts of critical mineral extraction
are not shared equally around the world.
Injustices in this area reflect the unfair and
disproportional shares of the benefits and
harms of extracting, processing, and trading
critical minerals. Often, the poor, vulnerable
and marginalised communities and nations
suffer the most from environmental damages,
socio-economic harms, and health risks, while
more privileged and richer groups and nations
enjoy the benefits.
Economic injustices
Economic injustice occurs when the financial
benefits, wealth and resources resulting
from the extraction of critical minerals
disproportionately flow to certain socio-
economically and politically privileged
groups, mostly in wealthy communities
and advanced economies while the already
poor and vulnerable populations bear the
economic costs in the form of environmental
and livelihood quality degradation, pollution,
land dispossession, poor working conditions,
health risks,128 and economic dependency.
Evidence shows that the wealth, revenue
and economic benefits from critical minerals
extraction, processing, and trade are
not evenly distributed. Rich groups and
powerful countries are gaining most, while
less underprivileged communities and less
developed nations face the lasting negative
consequences. For example, even though
Namibia, Zambia, and the DRC hold over 30%
of the world’s critical mineral deposits, most
of the profits go to multinational corporations
and mining companies in the Global North.4
China, with fewer reserves, earns more
from Critical Minerals than these countries
combined.4
This unfair distribution of critical minerals'
wealth is worsened by the fact that wealthy
countries dominate the high-value parts of the
supply chain (such as refining, manufacturing,
and technology) while poorer, mineral-rich
countries are mostly limited to raw materials
extraction. This is also because these
countries lack the technology, skilled labor,
infrastructure, and capital needed to add
more value to their extracted resources. As

deep labor exploitation, systematically

economically poor, resource-rich countries,

costs of critical minerals extraction.
Environmental injustices
Environmental injustices refer to the
disproportional and unfair distribution of the
environmental harms and benefits of critical
minerals extraction and processing; while the
negative impacts disproportionately affect
marginalized communities and the Global
South nations, the environmental benefits are
largely enjoyed by wealthier populations and
the Global North nations. The environmental
impacts of critical mineral extraction fall
most heavily on low-income countries, while
the benefits—such as lower carbon emissions
and cleaner air—mainly go to developed
countries in the Global North. This is a clear
case of environmental injustice, one of the
most visible and harmful forms of inequality
in critical minerals extraction that remains
highly overlooked by environmental, climate,
and sustainability campaigns and narratives
that are normally led by the more politically
and economically powerful nations. Mining
for critical minerals often causes serious
environmental damage, including water
pollution, deforestation, loss of biodiversity,
soil erosion, and the depletion of natural
resources at top of the critical minerals
supply chain. So, local communities in the
mining regions are often left to suffer the
consequences of the global energy and
digital transition which is supposed to bring
sustainability for all. In regions with weak
environmental laws and poor enforcement,
mining companies are rarely held accountable.
Corruption exacerbates the situation. While
the environmental benefits of reducing
greenhouse gases through green technologies
is global, wealthy nations are the main
beneficiaries of the local environmental
benefits of adopting these technologies
including, but not limited to less air, water, and
soil pollution, deforestation, and biodiversity
loss. In this case, sustainability for the Global
North comes at the cost of environmental
destruction borne by countries in the Global
South. This reflects deeper global inequalities
and highlights the paradox of the global
energy, digital, and sustainability transitions:
although it aims to fight climate change,
biodiversity loss, and pollution, it often creates
or worsens environmental injustice.

Social Injustices
Social injustices in critical minerals supply
chains refer to the ways in which the
extraction, processing, and global trade
exacerbate social inequalities, undermine
community rights, and exploit vulnerable
populations in the underprivileged
communities and the Global South while
contributing to social enhancements in
the more privileged communities and the
Global North. These injustices are especially
pronounced among groups already facing
systemic marginalization, such as women,
children, Indigenous peoples, and rural
communities. These vulnerable groups
experience the most severe consequences of
critical minerals extraction activities.
For instance, women and girls, are often the
primary caretakers responsible for accessing
water and managing household nutrition. As
a result, they are disproportionately exposed
to and affected by water pollution caused by
mining, which not only threatens their health
but also increases their caregiving burdens.
Children, too, are deeply impacted by polluted
environments and declining agricultural
productivity compromise food security, leading
to stunted growth and underdevelopment of
children due to malnutrition. Simultaneously,
Indigenous and rural communities in mining
zones regularly face forced displacement,
land dispossession, and the loss of their
cultural and spiritual heritage because mining
operations often encroach on ancestral lands
without the free, prior, and informed consent
(FPIC) of affected populations. In many cases
too, relocation and resettlements are carried
out without adequate compensation or viable
alternatives for livelihoods.
Beyond the economic toll, these displacements
sever local communities’ ties to sacred
sites such as shrines, ancestral burial
grounds, and sacred rivers, leading to deep
psychological and cultural trauma. Examples
from countries like the DRC, Bolivia, and
Argentina highlight these injustices. In these
regions, mining activities have displaced
communities, sometimes with the backing
of state authorities.130–133 In several instance
when residents resisted relocation, they
were threatened and told to accept meager
compensation or face eviction without any
restitution.130,131
In many regions, the extraction of critical
minerals leads to eroding and, in some cases,
disrupting of historical and socio-cultural
sites and ties with the people.4,134 The loss
of spiritual and cultural sites contributes to
long-lasting social and psychological harm
an important but often-overlooked impact
of extractive industries.76,123 Additionally,
mining activities have led to the destruction
of farmlands, contamination of water bodies,
and displacement of communities that depend
on agriculture, fishing, and livestock. This
undermines not only local economies but also
the social cohesion and cultural identity of
affected populations.
Social injustice compounds and intersect
with other dimensions of discriminations to
undermine health and people’s agency to
lead healthy lives.128 These practices reflect
a pattern where local communities bear
the brunt of mining's social costs, while the
wealthier groups and nations are enjoying
the social benefits associated with improved
quality of life and living conditions, reduced
pollution, and environmental and health
improvements. In this sense, despite being
framed as a pathway to a sustainable future,
the current approach to the energy, digital and
sustainability transitions can socially backfire
and contribute to social inequalities around
the world.

energy, digital, and sustainability transitions—
while intended to combat climate change,
secure sustainability for all, and leave no one
behind—can also produce new layers of health
disparities for already vulnerable populations.

This UNU-INWEH report highlights a major,
overlooked contradiction in the global energy,
digital, and sustainability transitions. The
demand for critical minerals such as lithium,
cobalt, copper, and rare earth elements has
risen sharply, tripling since 2010 and projected
to quadruple by 2050. These minerals are
indispensable for renewable energy systems,
electric mobility, and digital technologies
needed to meet climate goals and support
the Fourth Industrial Revolution. Yet, their
extraction is producing significant socio-
economic, environmental, and health costs.
Lithium mining in arid areas, for example,
consumes volumes of water equivalent to
the needs of millions of people each year,
leading to aquifer depletion, and freshwater
contamination. These outcomes affect food
security and population health, with women,
children, and Indigenous peoples bearing
disproportionate burdens. What makes the
expansion of extractive activities questionable
is that the benefits and costs of extracting
critical minerals are not distributed evenly. The
richer parts of the world and more privileged
population groups are normally the main
beneficiaries of extractive activities while the
poorer parts of the world and less privileged
communities are suffering from their costs.
This raises major justice and equity questions
that remain unanswered and ignored.
Without targeted intervention, the energy,
digital, and sustainability transitions risk
reproducing extractive colonial patterns,
creating “sacrifice zones” where marginalized
local communities bear the costs while
benefits largely accrue elsewhere. This
dynamic undermines progress on key
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs),
including water and sanitation (SDG 6),
health and wellbeing (SDG 3), poverty
reduction (SDG 1), and inequality (SDG 10),
and further contributes to supply chain
vulnerabilities and geopolitical risks. A Just
Transition requires a shift from exploitative
extractive practices toward arrangements
that embed accountability and equity across
the full life cycle of mineral production. This
includes ensuring zero net water depletion
in extraction, enabling community co-
governance of resources, and establishing
binding health reparations for affected
populations. Advancing these measures will
be critical to ensuring that the global energy
and digital revolutions deliver not only climate,
technological, and computational benefits, but
also justice, equity, and long-term resilience
and sustainability. The evidence presented
throughout this report substantiates three
critical and interconnected conclusions.

To mitigate the injustices and ensure a
balanced global transition to sustainability,
immediate actions are necessary around
key pillars: governance, sustainability,
equity, health and social disparities, and
collaboration.  Specific policies must be
tailored to communities, consumers, national
and local governments in the Global South,
governments in developed and industrialised
economies, researchers, and media to address
their unique roles and responsibilities.
Implementing these policies require urgent,
coordinated action, with governments
prioritizing policy reforms, corporations
committing to ethical practices, and
international bodies facilitating knowledge-

minerals agenda and transform the global
digital, energy, and sustainability transitions
into a truly inclusive pathway to sustainability,
where no one is left behind.

on high-risk mineral supply chains. Public
procurement policies should be utilized
to prioritize products containing recycled
content, boosting the commercial viability
of recovery technologies.
IV.	 Support Recycling and Circular Economy
Initiatives: Global recycling programs
must be promoted to decrease reliance
on primary extraction, with consumers,
especially in the Global North, advocating
for policies that mandate ethical sourcing
labels on products containing critical
minerals.
V.	 Mandate Transparent Product Information
and Guarantee the Right to Repair:
Consumer choices must be empowered by
making the content and history of products
visible and understandable. Governments
should also enact policies that require
manufacturers to provide consumers with
affordable repair services, spare parts,
and repair manuals for the full lifespan of
products.
G.	 Generating Knowledge and Sharing Data
Policies should be in place to ensure scientific
knowledge is translated into actionable
political change by fostering interdisciplinary
research on mining impacts. It mandates open
data sharing and community-led monitoring
to equip advocates with evidence, while
empowering media to critically challenge
corporate sustainability claims.
I.
Generate Interdisciplinary Knowledge for
Actionable Policy: Academic institutions
and researchers must prioritize integrated,
cross-disciplinary research on the water-
minerals nexus that holistically examines
the links between mining, health, and
justice, and actively translate complex
scientific findings into clear, actionable
policy recommendations for governments
and international bodies.
II.	 Promote Research on Supply Chain Risks
and Impacts: Research must be conducted
on comprehensive assessments across full
supply chains and lifecycles, evaluation
of trade-offs, highlighting various forms of
injustices and inequalities along the supply
chains, and innovations to address such
issues.
III.	 Institutionalize Open Data and Community-
Owned Evidence: Research institutions
must commit to open data standards for all
pollution and health information, ensuring
findings are easily accessible to affected
communities and advocates. Furthermore,
training communities to collect and
monitor their own environmental data
empowers them to provide respected,
localized evidence in legal and policy
debates.
IV.	 Amplify Local Struggles and Challenge
Dominant Narratives: Civil society,
Media and NGOs must prioritize in-depth
investigations into mining's social and
environmental impacts, tracing supply
chains to connect consumer demand
with local harm and actively challenging
the notion that resource extraction is an
unavoidable cost of the energy, digital
and sustainability transitions, while
providing media training to empower
community storytelling. They should
amplify voices from mining communities
to hold corporations accountable, while
researchers collaborate on policy tools for
diverse supply chains.

About UNU-INWEH
The United Nations University Institute for Water, Environment and Health
(UNU-INWEH) is one of 13 institutions that make up the United Nations
University (UNU), the academic arm of the United Nations. Known as 'The UN’s
Think Tank on Water', UNU-INWEH addresses critical water, environmental,
and health challenges around the world. Through research, training, capacity
development, and knowledge dissemination, the institute contributes to
solving pressing global sustainability and human security issues of concern
to the UN and its Member States. Headquartered in Richmond Hill, Ontario,
UNU-INWEH has been hosted and supported by the Government of Canada
since 1996. With a global mandate and extensive partnerships across UN
entities, international organizations, and governments, UNU-INWEH operates
through its UNU Hubs in Calgary, Hamburg, New York, Lund, and Pretoria,
and an international network of affiliates.
