Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has expanded significantly in recent years, permeating various
sectors of the economy and the daily lives of over 5.5 billion active internet users worldwide.
However, this rapid adoption necessitates an analysis of the trade-offs inherent to its operation,
many of which remain largely unknown to the public. Drawing on data from academic articles,
technical reports, data repositories, and government documents, this article examines the
physical, energy, and geopolitical dimensions underpinning AI. Although often perceived as
immaterial, AI depends on a vast and complex physical infrastructure, sustained by data centers
that house thousands of devices composed of a diverse array of minerals and metals, many
classified as critical. Currently, approximately 12,000 data centers are in operation globally,
including 992 hyperscale facilities spanning thousands of square meters. The short lifecycle of
equipment in these centers, combined with inadequate disposal practices, leads to the loss of
valuable metals from the supply chain, further intensifying mineral extraction and exacerbating
socio-environmental impacts. Simultaneously, the rivalry between the United States and China
over control of critical minerals and dominance in AI technologies has escalated geopolitical
tensions, resulting in mutual restrictions on the export of advanced technologies and essential
minerals. Another critical factor is AI’s substantial energy consumption: in the United States,
data centers already account for approximately 4% of national electricity consumption, with
projections reaching 9.1% by 2030. While major technology companies invest in renewable
energy sources such as solar and wind to meet this growing demand, these sources also require
significant volumes of critical minerals. These interrelated factors underscore the intricate
connections between Artificial Intelligence, Data Centers, Critical Minerals, Energy, and
Geopolitics.

Keywords: AI; Infrastructure; Energy Transition; Economy; Digital.

Introduction
In recent years, the term “Artificial Intelligence (AI)” has become one of the most
widely discussed topics in news reports and research worldwide (Maslej et al., 2024). Although
its definition is broad and complex - reflecting the diversity of fields it encompasses and the
different approaches used to understand and apply the concept (Sheikh et al., 2023) - most
interpretations converge on the idea that human-developed machines can not only perform
exhaustive tasks but also acquire intellectual capabilities similar to those of humans (Jiang et
al., 2022). This includes executing complex activities such as learning, reasoning, and decision-
making (Lake et al., 2016).
A striking example of this growth is ChatGPT, a chatbot developed by the U.S.-based
company OpenAI. Designed to facilitate communication and problem-solving through
interactive and dynamic conversations, ChatGPT became the fastest-growing internet
application in history, reaching 100 million users within just two months of its launch at the end
of 2022 (Milmo, 2023).
Since then, artificial intelligence has become deeply integrated into daily life (Devlin et
al., 2023; Maslej et al., 2024), with nearly every sector of the economy adopting the technology
to enhance efficiency and improve outcomes (Rashid & Kausik, 2024). AI applications now
range from robots in customer service call centers and navigation apps that provide optimized
routes and real-time traffic updates to social media algorithms that personalize content, hyper-
realistic videos and images that blur the line between reality and artificiality, autonomous
vehicles, translation tools, and academic text correction software, among countless other uses.
Accompanying this wave of growth, market projections indicate a rapid expansion of
the AI sector, with estimates predicting an increase from approximately $90 billion in 2020 to
$250 billion in 2025, and reaching around $830 billion by 2030 (Statista, 2024a).
This recent surge in AI development and widespread adoption across various sectors has
been driven, among other factors, by a significant increase in computational power (Hwang,
2018) and the vast availability of data that serves as the foundation for training AI models (Zha
et al., 2023). A report by OpenAI, for example, revealed that between 2012 and 2018, the
computational power used for AI training doubled every 3.4 months, resulting in a 300,000-
fold increase over that period (Amodei & Hernandez, 2018).
However, as AI adoption continues to expand, it is essential to examine the complex
interrelationship between Data Centers, Critical Minerals, Energy, and Geopolitics - the

foundational elements that sustain artificial intelligence - and the multiple trade-offs associated
with its operation, many of which remain largely unknown to the general public. As a society,
we must confront, challenge, or seek solutions to these issues, particularly if we intend to pursue
the widespread expansion of this technology.
Motivated by these concerns, this article first examines the physical infrastructure that
supports artificial intelligence, emphasizing the significant increase in the number and capacity
of data centers. These facilities house millions of essential components needed to meet growing
demands for computational power and data storage, underscoring their reliance on a wide range
of critical minerals and metals. Next, the article explores recent geopolitical developments
related to competition for dominance in AI development and control over the critical minerals
necessary for building this technological infrastructure, with a focus on the rivalry between the
United States and China. The third section addresses the high energy intensity of AI, which
significantly surpasses that of traditional digital applications, presenting global and national
estimates of energy consumption driven by this technology. Finally, the article analyzes the
interplay between AI’s increasing energy consumption and the energy transition, highlighting
how renewable energy sources such as wind and solar - often promoted as sustainable
alternatives - also depend heavily on critical minerals, reinforcing AI’s direct and indirect
reliance on these resources.

Methodology
To examine and discuss the relationship between Data Centers, Critical Minerals,
Energy, and Geopolitics as the foundational elements of Artificial Intelligence, this research is
based on a comprehensive collection and analysis of data from diverse scientific sources,
including academic articles, technical reports, data repositories, government documents, and
investigative journalism reports.
The complex components that make up the physical infrastructure of artificial
intelligence were detailed using the reports "The AI Disruption: Challenges and Guidance for
Data Center Design" (Avelar et al., 2023) and "Designing Data Centers for AI Clusters"
(Chatterjee & Venugopal, 2023), developed by specialized companies in the field.
To quantify the number of data centers worldwide, identify those classified as
hyperscale, and assess the growth of these infrastructures over time, data from the German
consulting firm Statista (2024b; 2024c; 2024d; 2024e) were utilized. Examples of hyperscale

by Chinese and American researchers, as well as official documents from the U.S. government

The Physical Infrastructure of Artificial Intelligence: Data Centers and Minerals
The frequent mention of Artificial Intelligence in news reports, often accompanied by
abstract terms such as Cloud, Big Data, Algorithms, Machine Learning, and Virtualization, can
create the misleading impression that these technologies operate immaterially, detached from
any physical infrastructure.
However, behind these terms lies a vast and complex network of physical systems,
primarily housed in data centers. These facilities are designed to accommodate and operate
thousands of servers organized in racks, alongside storage systems (such as SSDs and HDDs),

networking devices (switches, routers, and firewalls), processing units (CPUs, GPUs, and
TPUs), power supply and backup systems (generators and UPS), cooling mechanisms (fans,
chillers, and liquid cooling), environmental monitoring sensors, connection cables, and various
other essential components (Avelar et al., 2023; Chatterjee & Venugopal, 2023).
Data centers serve as the backbone of an AI-driven world, providing the critical
infrastructure necessary to meet the increasing demands for computational power and data
storage. The construction of these facilities depends on a broad range of minerals and metals -
including gallium, germanium, metallic silicon, tantalum, platinum-group metals, copper, rare
earth elements, silver, and gold - ranging from abundant materials to highly critical raw
resources (Pehlken et al., 2019; Crawford, 2021; Robbins & Van-Wynsberghe, 2022).
These resources undergo extensive extraction and refining processes to achieve the high
levels of purity required for the electronic, magnetic, mechanical, and optical properties
essential to AI systems (United Nations, 2024). It is estimated that the production of complex
electronic devices consumes between 50 and 350 times the final weight of the product in raw
materials (Ademe, 2021). For instance, manufacturing a laptop requires mobilizing
approximately 600 kg of materials, while an internet router demands around 500 kg (Ademe,
2021).
Currently, there are approximately 12,000 data centers worldwide (Statista, 2024b), of
which 992 are classified as hyperscale (Statista, 2024c). Although no universally precise
definition exists, IBM - a multinational technology company - suggests that a data center must
house at least 5,000 servers and span more than 10,000 square feet (approximately 929 m²) to
be considered hyperscale (Powell & Smalley, 2024). However, the largest data centers far
exceed this scale. A striking example is The Citadel Campus in Reno, Nevada, USA. Operated
by the American company Switch, which specializes in designing, building, and managing
large-scale data centers, this facility spans 7.2 million square feet (approximately 669,000 m²
or 66.9 hectares) (Switch, 2024). These figures highlight the enormous volume of minerals
required to sustain this critical infrastructure.

This growing demand for mineral raw materials to sustain Artificial Intelligence is
expected to rise continuously, paralleling the significant increase in the number of data centers.
Hyperscale data centers, for instance, have quadrupled over the past eight years, growing from
259 in 2015 to 992 in 2023 (Statista, 2024c). Projections indicate that this expansion will persist
in the coming years (Statista, 2024b; 2024d), driving a market valued at hundreds of billions of
dollars (Statista, 2024a; 2024d; 2024e).
To date, no detailed studies have precisely quantified the volume of minerals used in the
manufacturing and maintenance of data center infrastructure, nor their relationship with global
resource availability. However, supply difficulties for certain minerals have already been
observed (Areddy & Follow, 2023; Bradsher, 2024), particularly due to geopolitical and
strategic factors (Kalantzakos, 2020; Nakano, 2021; Xinyue, 2024; Zhou, 2024). This issue will
be further explored in the next section.
Another factor exacerbating this challenge is the high turnover rate of the hardware
infrastructure supporting artificial intelligence, driven by rapid technological advancements
(United Nations, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Many data center components have a limited lifespan

2024f). In addition to posing potential risks to human health and the environment due to the
presence of hazardous or toxic materials, this disposal process removes valuable metals from
the supply chain (Wang et al., 2024), necessitating the extraction of new raw materials to
compensate for these losses.

Minerals, Artificial Intelligence, and Geopolitics
In recent years, it has become increasingly evident that the supply of critical minerals
essential for artificial intelligence infrastructure is not dictated solely by market dynamics but
is also deeply shaped by geopolitical factors. The availability of these minerals, along with the
technologies associated with them, has become a central element in the global struggle for
supremacy between the United States and China (Lee, 2018; Kalantzakos, 2020; Nakano, 2021;
Xinyue, 2024; Zhou, 2024), triggering intense confrontations.
Currently, China dominates the market for critical minerals essential to AI infrastructure,
including antimony, gallium, germanium, and rare earth elements (Areddy & Follow, 2023;
Xinyue, 2024). In contrast, the United States leads in the manufacturing stage of AI-related
products - a process that relies heavily on advanced expertise in areas such as intellectual
property, design, automation, and fabrication technology (Xinyue, 2024).
Amid an escalating technological, economic, and political rivalry - which began at least
as early as 2018 during the Trump administration and intensified under Joe Biden - the U.S.
government announced measures in October 2022 aimed at restricting the export of advanced
technologies to China (Reuters, 2023). These actions included prohibiting the sale of advanced
chips and imposing export restrictions on semiconductor manufacturing equipment (United
States, 2022).
In July 2023, in response to these restrictions, China imposed limits on the export of
two essential minerals for AI-related technologies: gallium and germanium, of which it

accounts for approximately 90% and 60% of global production, respectively (Areddy & Follow,
2023).
By October 2023, the United States further tightened its restrictions on AI chip exports,
requiring the American company Nvidia to halt shipments to China of models designed to
comply with previous regulations, featuring specific performance limitations for the Chinese
market (Hoskins, 2023). Subsequently, in November 2024, the U.S. government expanded these
measures, mandating that the Taiwanese multinational Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing
Company (TSMC) also cease shipments of advanced chips intended for AI applications in
China (Freifeld & Potkin, 2024). Together, Nvidia and TSMC dominate the global market for
high-efficiency chips designed for artificial intelligence (Statista, 2024g).
In retaliation, in October 2024, the Chinese government escalated its restrictions on the
export of rare earth metals and other strategic minerals crucial for advanced technologies,
particularly targeting semiconductor manufacturers (Bradsher, 2024).
Amid these disputes, both the European Union and the United States are seeking new
strategies to secure access to critical minerals essential for technological development (Zhou,
2024). In the case of the United States, the White House has launched a series of initiatives -
estimated at over $120 billion - aimed at strengthening national supply chains for critical
minerals to reduce dependence on China (The White House, 2024).
As this article was being finalized, Donald Trump was inaugurated on January 20, 2025,
for his second (non-consecutive) term as President of the United States. On his second day in
office, he met at the White House with executives from major artificial intelligence companies
to announce a series of legislative restructurings and private investments totaling $500 billion,
aimed at financing AI infrastructure (Friesen, 2025; Holland, 2025). During the event, Larry
Ellison, an executive at Oracle Corporation - an American technology and computing company
- announced the construction of twenty new data centers, each spanning half a million square
feet (approximately 46,500 m²) (Holland, 2025). In an official statement, the White House
declared that these measures were designed to reinforce the United States’ global dominance in
artificial intelligence, making American leadership in AI a priority and promoting "human
flourishing, economic competitiveness, and national security" (The White House, 2025).
Less than a week after this announcement, the relatively unknown Chinese company
DeepSeek launched the DeepSeek-R1, an artificial intelligence chatbot developed to compete
with OpenAI’s ChatGPT. Built at a significantly lower cost than its competitors while delivering

These events underscore the strategic role of artificial intelligence and critical minerals
in contemporary global geopolitics, suggesting that further significant developments are likely
to unfold in the coming years.

Energy Consumption of Artificial Intelligence and Data Centers
Artificial intelligence models generally consume significantly more energy than
traditional applications such as email access, data retrieval, communication, or content
streaming (EPRI, 2024; Luccioni et al., 2024a). This high energy demand arises from the
reliance of modern large language models (LLMs) on complex neural networks with billions of
parameters, which are fine-tuned during training to recognize patterns and contextual
relationships within vast datasets (Naveed et al., 2023). Additionally, the management and
transfer of these massive datasets across thousands of physical infrastructure components - such
as memory units and processors, typically housed in data centers - further amplify the
substantial energy requirements of artificial intelligence processes (Bourzac, 2024).
For instance, a single query in OpenAI’s ChatGPT consumes approximately ten times
more electricity than a traditional search on Google (EPRI, 2024). Emerging AI-driven
technologies, such as AI-generated audio, images, and videos, exhibit even higher energy
consumption. In a series of experiments measuring the energy usage of AI models across
different tasks, Luccioni et al. (2024b) found that the most energy-intensive image generation
model consumed approximately 1,600 Wh (watt-hours) to create 1,000 high-definition images
- equivalent to 70 smartphone charging cycles.

Since the successful launch of ChatGPT in late 2022 - reaching 100 million users within
just two months (Milmo, 2023) - Big Tech companies have increasingly integrated AI into a
growing number of online interactions and digital tools (Vries, 2023). With 5.52 billion active
internet users worldwide (Statista, 2024h) engaging more frequently with AI-powered
technologies, the energy consumption associated with these interactions continues to escalate.
While some researchers argue that advancements in hardware have historically been
accompanied by improvements in energy efficiency - playing a key role in mitigating the rapid
rise in energy demand (Desislavov et al., 2023; Bourzac, 2024) - progress in AI-specific
processors appears to challenge this trend. Moreover, significant architectural changes in AI
GPUs are not expected in the short term due to high costs, complexity, and lengthy development
cycles (Bourzac, 2024). As a result, although new processors achieve higher performance per
watt compared to their predecessors, their increased capacity and large-scale deployment have
contributed to a continuous rise in overall energy consumption (Kindig, 2024; United Nations,
2024). Additionally, Jevons’ Paradox (Alcott, 2005) suggests that efficiency improvements can
paradoxically lead to an increase in total energy usage over time (Coskun, 2024).
As a consequence, the International Energy Agency (IEA, 2024) - a leading authority
on global energy trends - estimates that data centers, cryptocurrencies, and artificial intelligence
collectively consumed approximately 460 TWh of electricity in 2022, accounting for about 2%
of global electricity demand. The same report projects that this figure could double by 2026,
reaching between 620 and 1,050 TWh. At the lower estimate, this increase would be equivalent
to the annual electricity consumption of Sweden, while at the higher estimate, it would match
that of Germany (IEA, 2024).
In countries with a high concentration of these infrastructures, energy consumption is
already substantial. The United States, which has the largest number of data centers in the world
(Statista, 2024b), sees these facilities account for approximately 4% of national electricity
demand (IEA, 2024; EPRI, 2024). Driven in part by AI advancements, this share is projected
to rise to 6% by 2026 (IEA, 2024) and up to 9.1% by 2030 (EPRI, 2024). In China, electricity
demand from data centers is expected to reach approximately 300 TWh in 2026 and 400 TWh
by 2030 (IEA, 2024). Meanwhile, in Ireland - a country that offers tax incentives for data center
installations - these facilities already account for approximately 21% of the nation’s total
electricity consumption, with projections indicating an increase to 31% within the next three

Energy Transition and Minerals in the Context of Artificial Intelligence
Major technology companies have been intensifying their investments in renewable
energy sources as part of sustainability-driven strategies. These initiatives aim not only to
reduce their carbon footprint but also to enhance their corporate image among the public and
investors, particularly amid the growing global debate on energy transition (Thompson, 2024).
Although renewable energy is not always directly used in operations, these companies often
sign contracts to purchase clean energy in volumes equivalent to their consumption, thereby
contributing to the global energy transition (BNEF, 2023). For example, Google and Amazon
both claim to offset 100% of their energy use - including that of their data centers - through
corporate power purchase agreements (Amazon, 2024a; Google, 2024b), while Microsoft has
committed to reaching this goal by 2030 (Welsch, 2022).
To achieve these targets, Amazon reports owning or partnering in approximately 500
solar and wind projects worldwide (Amazon, 2024a). Meanwhile, Google stated that between
2010 and 2023 it signed over 115 clean energy purchase agreements, corresponding to a
generation capacity equivalent to approximately 36 million solar panels (Google, 2024b). In
2024, Google also announced that its future data centers would be strategically located near
solar and wind farms to ensure direct energy supply from these renewable sources (Calma,
2024a). That same year, Microsoft revealed the signing of one of the largest corporate future
renewable energy procurement contracts, valued at approximately $17 billion (Calma, 2024b).
As demonstrated in Global Geography of the Energy Transition and Mineral Extraction
(Stacciarini & Gonçalves, 2025b), this trend has intensified largely due to the increasing
economic viability of solar and wind energy, whose costs have dropped drastically over the past
decade, making them highly competitive in various regions (Statista, 2024i; Statista, 2024j).
Between 2013 and 2022, solar (47.8%) and wind (38.7%) energy accounted for approximately

86.5% of the $3.4 trillion invested in global renewable energy infrastructure (IRENA & CPI,
2023).
However, similar to chips and other data center components, solar and wind energy
technologies depend heavily on critical minerals (WB, 2020; IEA, 2021; IRENA, 2021;
Stacciarini & Gonçalves, 2025b). This dependency stems from intrinsic technological
characteristics, such as lower energy density. For example, wind turbines generate energy more
gradually compared to fossil fuel-powered turbines, requiring larger infrastructure and,
consequently, greater use of mineral resources to achieve equivalent energy production levels
(Stacciarini & Gonçalves, 2025b).
Data from the International Energy Agency (IEA, 2021) indicate that an onshore wind
power plant can consume up to nine times more mineral resources than a natural gas plant of
similar capacity. In offshore wind projects - where Amazon claims to be the world’s largest
corporate buyer of energy (Amazon, 2024b) - critical mineral consumption can be up to fifteen
times higher, due to the need for large foundation piles, underwater structures, and extensive
networks of submarine cables and pipelines.
The issue of energy density also applies to solar panels. Modern solar farms often consist
of millions of panels, and because this technology relies on a significant set of critical minerals
(WB, 2020; IEA, 2021; IRENA, 2021; Stacciarini & Gonçalves, 2025b), pressure on mineral
extraction continues to grow (Stacciarini & Gonçalves, 2025a). A notable example is the Orion
Solar Belt complex in Texas, designed to supply power to Google’s data centers. With over 1.3
million solar panels and a generation capacity of 900 MW (SP Energy, 2024), a proportional
analysis suggests that to meet the projected 35 GW energy demand of U.S. data centers by 2030
(Newmark, 2024), approximately 50 million solar panels would be needed if the entire energy
supply were sourced from solar power.
Another challenge is the significantly shorter lifespan of solar and wind installations
compared to traditional power plants. While coal-fired or nuclear power plants can operate for
40 to 50 years, solar installations are estimated to last 25 to 30 years, and wind farms 20 to 25
years (Statista, 2024k). This more frequent replacement cycle, combined with the limitations of
recycling processes for these structures (Chowdhury et al., 2020; Jensen et al., 2020; Massoud
et al., 2023), further intensifies the demand for minerals.

Conclusions
This study analyzed the complex and often overlooked interconnection between Data
Centers, Critical Minerals, Energy, and Geopolitics as the foundational elements of Artificial
Intelligence. Initially, it highlighted how AI - currently driving approximately $250 billion
annually, with projections reaching $830 billion by 2030 - has become a central element in
global debates, integrating deeply into various aspects of daily life.
Although often perceived as immaterial, this study demonstrated that artificial
intelligence depends on a vast and intricate physical infrastructure, primarily concentrated in
data centers. These facilities house essential components such as servers, storage systems,
processing units, and cooling mechanisms, all designed to meet the growing demands for
computational power and data storage. The production of these systems requires a broad range
of minerals and metals, many of which are classified as critical. For instance, manufacturing a
single internet router can necessitate the mobilization of up to 500 kg of raw materials.
Another critical issue identified is the short lifecycle of data center equipment, typically
ranging from two to five years. The improper disposal of these devices - without adequate
recycling processes - not only removes valuable metals from the supply chain, increasing the
need for primary extraction through mining, but also poses significant risks to human health
and the environment due to the presence of toxic substances. Furthermore, the extraction of
these resources is frequently associated with environmental, social, economic, and political
imbalances, particularly in regions of the Global South.
Data collected indicate that approximately 12,000 data centers are currently in operation
worldwide, with the number of large-scale hyperscale data centers having quadrupled in the
past eight years, now totaling 992 facilities. Projections suggest that this expansion will
continue in the coming years.
Although precise studies on the volume of minerals used in the construction and
maintenance of this infrastructure are still lacking, supply shortages of certain resources have
already been observed, exacerbated by geopolitical and strategic tensions. The ongoing
competition between the United States and China over the control of critical minerals and
leadership in AI-related technologies exemplifies these disputes. Since 2022, the U.S.
government has implemented a series of restrictions on the export of advanced technologies,
such as chips and semiconductors, to China, which, in response, has imposed limitations on the
export of essential minerals required for manufacturing AI-related technologies.

The study also underscored the high energy consumption associated with artificial
intelligence applications. As the number of users engaging with AI technologies continues to
rise, energy demand is expected to increase further. Currently, data centers, cryptocurrencies,
and AI collectively account for an estimated 2% of global electricity consumption - a proportion
that could double in the coming years. In the United States, data centers already represent
approximately 4% of national electricity use, with projections reaching 9.1% by 2030. In some
regions, local power grids are already experiencing strain due to the increasing energy demand.
Although major technology companies are investing in renewable energy sources such
as solar and wind to meet the electricity needs of their data centers and AI applications, these
sources also require substantial amounts of critical minerals due to intrinsic factors such as
lower energy density, shorter lifespan, and recycling limitations. For example, an onshore wind
power plant can consume up to nine times more minerals than a natural gas plant of similar
capacity, while offshore wind projects can demand up to fifteen times more. Large-scale
projects such as the Orion Solar Belt - which is installing over 1.3 million solar panels in Texas
(USA) to power Google’s data centers - illustrate this increasing reliance on critical minerals.
This body of data and analysis demonstrates that the expansion of AI and its associated

continues.
