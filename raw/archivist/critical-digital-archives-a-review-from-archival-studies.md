Critical Digital Archives: A Review from Archival Studies
ITZA A. CARBAJAL AND MICHELLE CASWELL
THE FOLLOWING SITUATION WILL sound familiar to those working in an academic archival
setting: A student or faculty member comes in for a research consultation with an archi-
vist and begins by stating they want to start a “digital archive.” The archivist might ﬁrst
ask a few questions and give a general overview of what building digital archives actu-
ally entails, from decisions around long-term digital preservation, to the creation of
metadata, to the ethics of online access. Realizing the enormity of the tasks behind
building and maintaining digital archives, the student, independent researcher, or faculty
member may leave feeling overwhelmed but also, ideally, more intent on giving their
project plan more detailed consideration.
As the preceding scenario implies, a deeper knowledge of digital archival theory
and practice—how records came to be in digital archives, the infrastructures that main-
tain them, and the tools necessary to give access to and context for them—isn’t ancillary
to historical work but provides important context to do digital history better. Historians’
relationship to archives used to be simple; they were users of collections painstakingly
appraised, processed, described, and made accessible by archivists. But with the coming
of the digital age and, in particular, the growth of digital humanities, historians’ relation-
ship to records has become more complicated. Historians now not only use but create,
contribute to, and theorize collections of digital records, some at a massive scale.1 In the
digital realm, the line between historian and archivist may seem, at times, blurry. In
light of this shift, this article urges historians to pay more attention to discussions sur-
rounding digital records and archival practices emerging from archival studies. More
speciﬁcally, this article identiﬁes and summarizes seven key themes and corresponding
debates about digital records in contemporary archival scholarship: (1) materiality, (2)
appraisal, (3) context, (4) use, (5) scale, (6) relationships, and (7) sustainability. We end

Archival studies is a subﬁeld of information studies that is concerned with records
as persistent representations of human activity that travel across space and time.2 The
subﬁeld’s primary theoretical concerns are deﬁnitions of records and “recordness”;
notions of evidence; conceptions of provenance; methodologies for determining historic
value; ways to create pathways for user retrieval of relevant materials; and issues sur-
rounding the representation of records through descriptive tools, such as ﬁnding aids
and catalogues.3 Readers must also note that for archival studies scholars, there is a dif-
ference between “the archive” invoked as a humanistic metaphor and the actual existing
physical and digital archives—always written with an s—the latter of which is the main
concern of archivists, archival studies scholars, and this article.4
Although once aligned closely with the discipline of history, the ﬁeld of archival
studies has shifted over the past few decades to form closer professional alliances with
library and information studies. Today, professional training for archivists generally
emerges out of master of library and information science (or information studies) pro-
grams located in schools of information, or iSchools.5 Over the past two decades, archi-
val studies has experienced a tremendous shift, as archivists have more critically interro-
gated their relationship to power and, for the most part, have embraced their active,
subjective roles in struggles for social justice.6 Over the past decade, archivists and ar-
chival studies scholars have redeﬁned and reshaped core tenets of theory and practice.
In a 2017 issue of the Journal of Critical Library and Information Studies, Michelle
Caswell, Ricardo Punzalan, and T-Kay Sangwand deﬁned “critical archival studies” as
“those approaches that (1) explain what is unjust with the current state of archival re-
search and practice, (2) posit practical goals for how such research and practice can and
should change, and/or (3) provide the norms for such critique.”7 We place ourselves
and our work explicitly within this movement toward a more critical archival studies.

In our opinion, it is crucial for historians working with and in digital domains to be
aware of and engage archival studies perspectives so as not to reinvent the proverbial
wheel on issues that scholars of archival studies and practicing archivists have deeply
engaged with for decades. A deeper knowledge of digital archival theory and practice—
how records came to be in digital archives, the infrastructures that maintain them, the
tools necessary to give access to and context for them, and the evolving principles and
motives—provides important context and guidance on how to do digital history better.
Indeed, we hope this article launches conversations and collaborations between archi-
vists and historians with the goal of creating more thoughtful, more sustainable, and
more critically engaged digital archives and digital history.
Before we delve into our exploration of the previously identiﬁed seven key themes,
a brief caveat is in order. What historians may refer to as “digital archives” encompass
two different types of collections of records in the minds of archivists: (1) born-digital
records (such as emails, Word documents, and tweets) that have been selectively col-
lected by archival institutions or organizations and preserved and (2) analogue records
(such as those created in paper, analogue ﬁlm, and other nondigital formats) that have
been selectively digitized, selectively collected by archival institutions or organizations,
and then selectively preserved. While, once digitized, formerly analogue materials pres-
ent the same technical, preservation, and ethical challenges as born-digital records, the
two types of records are conceptually distinct in the minds of archivists for a number of
reasons. For example, the context of the creation of a born-digital versus digitized ana-
logue record is different, as are subsequent strategies for collecting or capturing such
records in digital archival systems. Digitizing an analogue record fundamentally
changes the nature of the record itself, adding new layers of context and meaning and,
in the minds of some archival theorists, creating a record that is new and fundamentally
different from its analogue predecessor.8 Another consideration is that, unlike digitized
analogue records, born-digital records have no singular original referent and can be du-
plicated endlessly; no one, for example, has an original email record the same way they
might possess an original letter or manuscript. For simplicity’s sake, while we are
adopting the generic umbrella term of “digital archives” in this piece, we will point out
instances when the differences between born-digital and digitized records present im-
portant distinctions.

student at the University of Washington School of Information. Prior to starting her doc-

post-custodial projects in Latin America in collaboration with a number of international
partners. She received a master’s degree in information studies with a focus on digital
archival practice, public history, nonproﬁt management and public policy, and museum
studies. Research interests include children’s records and the ethical implications of
working with digital records, especially those documenting violent or difﬁcult historic
periods. Lastly, Carbajal identiﬁes as a US Latina born into the New Orleans Central
American diaspora and a daughter to Honduran parents of working-class backgrounds.

We begin with the topic of materiality and infrastructure. From an archival studies per-
spective, digital archives are material problems—that is, they warrant a serious commitment
to creating and maintaining material infrastructure. What many historians and digital hu-
manities scholars call a “digital archive” would make archivists balk; scanned documents
simply placed on a personal website, a hard drive, or—even worse from an archival per-
spective—a proprietary social media account do not constitute a “digital archive” unless
there is a plan for preserving them across space and time, maintaining the context of their
creation through metadata, and ensuring continual access to present and future users. For
archivists, preservation and description are key ingredients in making a collection of records
“archival.” Both of these archival functions require material infrastructure and labor.
For at least a few decades, scholars of information studies and science and technology
studies have cautioned against naïve claims that digital materials were free from material
constrictions.9 The humanistic formulation that frames the digital and the material as oppo-
sites ignores the material infrastructure that must be in place for anything digital to function.
Digitized records, for example, are scanned on equipment in ofﬁce spaces by human staff.
Once digitized, if they are placed online, the records must be stored in servers and travel
across international ﬁber-optic cables to be made widely accessible. And then they must be
viewed on material devices like computers or smartphones. As Madonna reminded us in
1985, we live in a material world, with no way to get around that materiality.
Over time, material objects demand maintenance, and maintenance requires infra-
structure.10 We may apply Shannon Mattern’s important work on libraries as infrastruc-
ture to archives such that we can see archives as a whole and digital archives speciﬁ-
cally “as a network of integrated, mutually reinforcing, evolving infrastructures—in
particular, architectural, technological, social, epistemological and ethical infra-
structures.”11 For example, digital preservation requires layers of infrastructure.12 Serv-

structure that can run checksums on ﬁles to detect corruption, that can convert obsolete
ﬁles to other formats, or that can emulate original formats if necessary. Similarly, archi-

context. If users do not know who created a record, or where or why, it becomes virtu-

crucial to its recordness. Let us not forget that without reliable and detailed metadata, it
also becomes virtually impossible to ﬁnd the desired record or collection of records
among a sea of digital ﬁles. Thus, when archivists promise to maintain the context of
records and make them discoverable over time, they are committing to building and
maintaining infrastructures—standards, software, hardware—that enable the creation,
maintenance, and sharing of metadata.
None of this would be possible without labor. It is people who digitize records, cap-
ture born-digital records, preserve them, and describe them. Their positions run the
gamut from tenured, professionally trained master’s degree–holding archivists to precari-
ously employed contract laborers and unpaid interns.13 In some cases, incarcerated people
conduct the labor of digitization and digital maintenance for well below minimum
wage.14 For their part, archivists have responded to this precarity with increasing calls for
advocacy and unionization.15 It would do us well to remember, whenever we access a
digital record, that people are behind it.
And ﬁnally, the material infrastructure and labor required by digital archives de-
mand resources—natural, human, and ﬁnancial. Resources are equitably distributed nei-
ther in society as a whole nor, unsurprisingly, in archives. Decisions about the alloca-
tion of resources determine how and what gets digitized, captured, preserved, described,
and made accessible. With inﬁnite resources, a near-inﬁnite digitization of records
would be possible (albeit not desirable by all); with very limited resources, a very lim-
ited digitization of records is possible. Thus, digital archives often reﬂect the perspec-
tives and serve the needs of the wealthiest in society at the expense of telling more accu-
rate, complicated, or diverse stories. Compare, for example, the scope of digital
archives made available by a well-resourced predominantly white institution like the
Getty Research Institute and those made available by a small independent community
archive representing people of color, like the South Asian American Digital Archive.16

All these material commitments, required infrastructures, resource allocations, and
labor decisions manifest layers of “ideas, values, and social responsibilities,” in Mat-
tern’s words.19 Digital archives reveal political commitments. They are not neutral.20 As
such, historians engaging in digital projects would do well to ask a speciﬁc set of ques-

infrastructures, and how? What are the consequences—ﬁnancial, human, and ecologi-

We need to focus on appraisal and silences. All archival collections are compendia
of silences—as Michel-Rolph Trouillot writes, not everything gets recorded, not all
records are collected by archives, not all archives are used to write stories, and not all
stories are used to tell grand narratives.21 For every record made available in archives,
countless others are not. Appraisal is the intellectual process by which archivists deter-
mine the enduring value of records offered to a repository. Appraisal is followed by se-
lection, the process by which archivists pick which records to keep based on the value
determined during appraisal.22
Notions of value are always contextual. They are embedded in the mission and poli-
cies of the particular archival repository for which the archivist works; the resources, la-
bor, and infrastructure made available for or constraining each project; the training and
philosophy of the archivist and the archives; the political, historical, and cultural milieu
in which the archivist works; and the archivist’s professional ethics and personal val-
ues.23 Through the determination of value made during the appraisal process, archivists
decide on what to keep and what to get rid of—what becomes raw materials of history
and collective memory and what will be gone forever. What remains in archives is “a
sliver of a sliver” of what happened.24
Likewise, for every record made available online, countless are not. In appraisal for
digitization, archivists must decide which records get digitized based on resources, pri-
orities, imagined user needs, preservation needs, and politics. Given the limited re-
sources made available to most archives, a small fraction of archival holdings gets digi-
tized and made available online. Similarly, collections of born-digital records are made
possible—or impossible—by corporate ﬁnancial interests that dictate, for example,
whether social media records are preserved at all or are available for capture by archiv-

Archival silences are magniﬁed in digitization projects. Decisions about which ana-
logue records to digitize and, conversely, which not to digitize are dictated by the
resources (in terms of stafﬁng, infrastructure, and ongoing digital preservation treat-
ment) that administrators and funders allocate to digitization projects. These decisions,
while often unmarked, add yet another layer of silence to the records that make their
way into digital archives. Thus, what users see in an ostensibly ﬁnalized digital reposi-
tory represents many layers of appraisal decisions, many records that did not make the
digitization cut, and, subsequently, an exponential multitude of silences. These silences,
as Trouillot would agree, aren’t random but rather replicate dominant power structures,
such that one cannot accurately talk about digital archives without addressing such
inequities. Because of this, each record in a digital repository represents both “an accre-
tion of meaning” and a “bundle of silence.”25 Silences are further obscured by the per-
ceived legitimacy of digital interfaces and the lack of face-to-face interaction between
archivist and user. At the same time, many subjects of records would resist the
unwanted attention of being represented so publicly in an online archives, freely acces-
sible to all. Thus, in working with digital records, historians would do well to ask the
following: Where is the accretion of meaning, and where is the bundle of silence? What
records made their way online, and why? What did not make its way into the digital
sliver, and why? How do digital interfaces construct historical legitimacy? How do they
obscure silences? When are digital silences the result of oppressive power structures
that delegitimize nondominant forms of knowledge production, and when are they acts
of refusal?
We then turn to conversations on context and description. In using and creating digi-
tal archives, metadata must also be considered a vital component of the overall digital
record. Metadata, commonly deﬁned as data about data, is the information that provides
context to an object, digital or analogue. Archivists, now better equipped with a broader
understanding of the needs of a variety of digital users and the systems available for
managing, storing, and accessing digital records, oftentimes achieve better and more
complex metadata standards than those previously used. For digital archival projects us-
ing or expanding on existing metadata, archivists may ﬁnd fruitful opportunities to eval-
uate the number of already existing problematic metadata derived from institutional col-
lections and in the overall archival ﬁeld, thus improving the overall user experience.
Metadata speaks about not only the content of a record but the context of its physical
and digital origin, structure, and technical makeup. Archivists and users depend on
metadata in order to ﬁnd, contextualize, discover, manage, analyze, search, design, and
distribute records in digital archives. Metadata is best understood when divided into
categories such as administrative, descriptive, technical, structural, and preservation.26
Administrative metadata deals with information such as copyright or sharing and access
permissions, often deﬁned by the creating party. Descriptive metadata includes informa-

The increase in access to more metadata in these digital archives also brings to the

alongside the actual physical and digital object. Staff in charge of digitization or digital

schemas; ﬂexible and adaptable structures; and language capabilities beyond the as-

metadata praxis as a separate but integral aspect of archival practice. In addition to the

We conclude with a proposed path toward critical digital archives. Given the current
state of the seven themes just highlighted, we see two possible divergent paths emerging
in the near future. The ﬁrst path represents the continuation of the status quo of digital
archival practice, leading to materially, ethically, and environmentally unsustainable
projects that contribute to the ongoing maldistribution of cultural and ﬁnancial re-
sources. This is not the future we wish to see. Instead, given the seven considerations
outlined previously and recent discourses on critical archival studies, we aim for a dif-
ferent path ahead—one that we call “critical digital archives.”70
Inspired by Caswell, Punzalan, and Sangwand’s deﬁnition of “critical archival studi-
es,” we envision critical digital archives emerging from those theories, practices, and
projects that critique the current state of digital archives and enact meaningful changes
to that state.71 Such critical digital projects work toward what Lae’l Hughes-Watkins
calls a “reparative archive,” and they hold archivists and other archival creators ethically
responsible for what they collect and make available digitally. A critical framework
begins by asking how digital archival projects may contribute to a more equitable distri-
bution of resources, empower oppressed communities to fully and complexly represent
themselves, dismantle systems of oppression as they impact archival records, and strive
to acknowledge and repair historical and ongoing harm toward people and the environ-
ment.72 If we start digital archival projects with these goals in mind, we end up with
digital archives radically different from what we currently have and futures radically
different from what the present trajectory suggests. We take heed here from Kim Chris-
ten and Jane Anderson’s work on slow archives, which they posit are “about focusing
differently, listening carefully, and acting ethically.”73 Focusing, listening, and acting
differently, with careful intent and care, can counteract the incessant need for new,
more, and bigger digital archives. Digital archives do not need to be approached hap-
hazardly in order to be used, enjoyed, and exposed.

tal archives in particular must adapt to technological changes, shifting expectations,
evolving scholarly practices, and larger social shifts.74 Historians and other scholars
