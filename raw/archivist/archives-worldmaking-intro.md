This paper introduces the special issue ‘Archives as worldmaking’. Moving beyond the traditional focus
on archives as tools of nation- or empire-building, the special issue posits archiving as a foundational
practice of political worldmaking. It therefore emphasises the mechanics of worldmaking, as much as its
diverse political manifestations. The paper ﬁrst provides an overview of recent scholarship on archives
and worldmaking within geography and related ﬁelds. It then presents the speciﬁc contributions to the
special issue, organised around the key themes of worldmaking's plurality, its situated nature, and its
connection to institution-building. The paper concludes by outlining four future research directions:
colonial logics, racial capitalism, misinformation, and the role of archivists.

Recent scholarship has seen growing interest in both archives and
worldmaking. Whiletheseconceptshavelong historiesacrossvarious
ﬁelds, this renewed attention largely stems from their utility in crit-
ically engaging with political power, particularly within colonial and
postcolonialsettings.Adom Getachew's Worldmakingafter Empire, for
instance, has spurred signiﬁcant interest, including among geogra-
phers.1 In these studies, worldmaking serves as shorthand for the
complex and often contested ways different groups constructed
shared geopolitical imaginaries, understandings that went beyond
the usual frameworks of nation-building or empire.
Simultaneously, the now well-established archival turn (associ-
ated with scholars like Ann Stoler, Achille Mbembe, and Antoinette
Burton) has prompted a critical re-examination of archiving prac-
tices.2 Many core tenets of this turndsuch as viewing archives as sites
of power, challenging objectivity, understanding the political con-
struction of knowledge, and focusing on absences and silencesdwill
be familiar to historical geographers. However, a notable develop-
ment is that this critical perspective has brought archives to the
attention ofscholarsworkingoutsidethe traditional archivaldomains
of history and historical geography.
The breadth of this contemporary engagement is showcased in
this special issue. We feature contributions from scholars working
across historical and political geography, political science, inter-
national relations, history, and anthropology, all united by a focus
on worldmaking. Like much recent work adopting the term, our
interest lies speciﬁcally in political worldmaking, extending its
application to a wider range of case studies, places, and time pe-
riods than previously explored within geography. Yet while the
politics of worldmaking have received attention, its mechanics
warrant closer examination. This collection addresses this gap by
giving equal weight to the making e the means, mechanisms, and
materials involved e alongside the worlds produced.
It is here, in understanding the making, that archives play a
crucial role. The central argument of this special issue is that
archiving should be understood as a form of worldmaking. Rather
than simply being repositories about the world, archival practices e
and contestations over them e actively shape possibilities for
thought, action, belonging, and place-making. This perspective
complicates the traditional interpretation of archives as corner-
stones of nation-building. The special issue's origins stemmed from
two sessions at the 2023 American Association of Geographers
Annual Meeting entitled ‘archives and internationalism’. Following

Worldmaking's appeal to us lies in its duality, which retains
sensitivity to large-scale geographical imaginaries (world) while
highlighting the active processes of construction (making). In this,
worldmaking has typically been used to centre alternatives to
modern statehood and empire. Although it could be interpreted
very broadly, then, scholars have deployed it in a more precise way
to critically interrogate the geopolitical imaginaries that shape past
and ongoing imperialisms and global circulations of capital. Prac-
tices of archiving e as the collection and curation of evidence
within contested regimes of legitimacy e are, as we show in this
special issue, integral to worldmaking in all its capacious in-
terpretations. This is evident in ongoing contestations over histor-
ical truth and over archival and museum collections within both
far-right regimes and warzones, where claims to territory and
legitimate authority over its representation are integral to battles
over historical materials.3
Therefore, this special issue emphatically investigates archives
as worldmaking, not merely archives and worldmaking as separate
topics. It foregrounds the processual, active, and ongoing ways ar-
chives are used to construct, normalise, stabilise, or challenge
geopolitical imaginaries. The papers consequently explore how
practices of archiving are deployed as political instruments and
examine the relationship between past political projects and pre-
sent struggles. In the remainder of this introduction, we brieﬂy
survey recent scholarship on both archives and worldmaking,
introduce the speciﬁc contributions within this special issue, and
conclude by suggesting four potential avenues for future research.
Archives and worldmaking
Scholarship within and beyond geography increasingly dem-
onstrates that archives are not neutral repositories. It is now a near-
axiomatic premise that encounters with archivesdand the prac-
tices of collection, curation, and display underpinning themdare
shaped by geographies of power. Historical geographers, whose
disciplinary positioning often necessitates a critical reﬂection on
methods, have been particularly engaged with these approaches. It
feels especially ﬁtting, therefore, that this special issue appears in
the 50th anniversary edition of the Journal of Historical Geography,
as archives represent one of the key sites of inquiry that unite
historical geographers across their diverse interests.
Building archives, and struggling against archival absences,
actively shape conﬁgurations of nation, region, and other geopo-
litical imaginaries. As Robert Gyori and Charles Withers argue, this
highlights the performative role that both geography and archival
materials play in nation-building.4 Echoing postcolonial scholar-
ship following Edward Said, this performative function underscores
how knowledge production practices are implicated in territorial
struggles. Consequently, battles over historical narrative and evi-
dencedoften centred on heritage and archivesdincreasingly
characterise efforts to legitimise political projects globally, some-
times violently, as Rosie Bsheer has argued.5 Parallels can be drawn
here with the wider politicisation of public memory; for instance,
the recent virtual special issue in this journal, ‘Contesting Monu-
ments’, revealed how heritage sites, much like archives, have
emerged as key battlegrounds for political struggle.6
The practice of archival research e long examined less as a
‘hydraulic’ process of ready-made stories waiting to be ‘tapped’
than as an epistemic encounter e also varies widely across the
spectrum of historical scholarship in and beyond geography.7 In
this, on the one hand, scholars like Anjali Arondekar emphasise the
need to read along the archival grain, so to speak, that is, to make
sense of the logic of the archive as a site where categories, terri-
tories, absences, and potential are equally articulated.8 Similarly,
Black Studies has drawn attention to the invention of political
worlds in practices of critical fabulation, which has also been taken
up within feminist geographical scholarship. In this work, the po-
wer dynamics that produce Black and colonised subjects as non-
speaking ﬁgures become loci for invention and reimagination.9
Archives are, in these readings, also sites of affective attachment
e there is desire for archival closure that is rarely met, and fantasies
of narrative that seldom play out.10
Against the backdrop of struggles over dominant or colonial
archives, efforts to establish and maintain counter-collections
showcase geographies of liberation, resistance, and refusal. These
projects are frequently bound to alternative visions of nationhood,
international peoplehoods, or pan-regional identities. Archives in
such practices performatively articulate e or interpellate, in the
Althusserian sense e community and territory in a variety of ways.
In Joan Nestle's effort to locate a lesbian archive within its com-
munity rather than behind the walls of an academic institution, she
writes about the archive's bringing to being a ‘lesbian people’.11 In
Elizabeth Povinelli's work on the Indigenous digitisation of
Australian maps, the rearticulation of territory through Indigenous
archival materials that include oral traditions reconﬁgures the
landscape, disarticulating it from colonial property relations and
borders.12 Conversely, projects of systematic archival erasure
starkly reveal the ways in which authoritarian and colonial states
weaponise archives to articulate new national narratives and ter-
ritorial constructions.13 To paraphrase Eunsong Kim, worldmaking,
in all its forms, is dictated by whose garbage counts as an archive.14

through which this special issue examines worldmaking. While the

term worldmaking has a history in ﬁelds like anthropology and
literary studies, it has recently gained traction among political ge-
ographers, inspired by Getachew's Worldmaking after Empire.15
Getachew reframes anti-colonial movements not merely as pur-
suits of national sovereignty, but as ambitious projects aimed at
constructing alternative global orders. Alongside the work of
scholars such as Achille Mbembe and Gary Wilder, this work re-
thinks the consolidation of state sovereignty in the mid-twentieth
century by highlighting alternative political trajectories at the
twilight of empire.16
In this context, worldmaking also resonates with related ideas. It
implies a move towards liberation that seeks to undo the colonial
‘worlding’ that Spivak identiﬁes and connects to Said's reading of
‘worldliness’ in articulating liberation as a project extending
beyond the conﬁnes of particular struggles.17 Worldmaking, un-
derstood in this way, is thus necessarily a geographical project e
involving the demarcation of territories, the establishment of
chronologies, and the embedding of memory and anticipation into
political consciousness. It is also a political project that unsettles
the colonial mapping of the world into distinct governable regions
and seeks to rearticulate a logic of global belonging outside of
empire or statehood.
Following Getachew, historical and political geographers have
employed worldmaking to analyse practices as diverse as borders,
diplomatic training, and labour internationalism.18 Geographers are
drawn to the concept's ideological openness, viewing it as distinct
from
but
related
to
ideas
such
as
internationalism,
self-
determination, and sovereignty. They are also, however, drawn to
its critical, anti-colonial subtext, representing what Amber Murrey
calls an ‘aspiration that entails the dismantling of imperial relations
and the reconstitution of global structures of power.’19 World-
making thus functions as a critical, analytical tool. It often serves to
complement and complicate analyses of nation-building while
retaining a sharper political edge than related concepts like
cosmopolitanism. It provides a framework for exploring multiple,
alternative, and sometimes subaltern, narratives that operate
beyond and against imperial or state-centric readings of political
life.20
Contributions
The papers in this special issue cover a wide variety of world-
making projects, mostly from the early 20th century to today, and
across localities as diverse as Poland, Sierra Leone, and Jordan. In
introducing the papers, three broad themes emerge. First is the
relationship
between
worldmaking
and
plurality.
The
papers
identify how worldmaking is rarely a singular or harmonious pro-
cess, but typically involves multiple, often overlapping and con-
ﬂicting, political worlds. Central questions emerge about whose
stories are told and whose ways of knowing are validated, not only
between dominant and resistant forms of worldmaking but also
within these categories. Alexander Manby's paper, for example,
focuses on the Naga communities in Northeast India, the ‘missing’
archival collection of the late Naga anticolonial nationalist leader
Angami Zapu Phizo, and efforts by Naga activists to construct an
alternative Naga ‘national’ archive in Nagaland. Manby shows how
competing archival claims reﬂect the multiplicity of potential Naga
geopolitical futures. Similarly, Jake Hodder explores Pan-African
archive building in the interwar United States to show how Black
activists and white philanthropists converged on archiving pro-
jects, while holding very different perspectives on the political
possibilities that such collections offered. Anand Sreekumar's paper
explores archives in the broader sense of a literary, published
archive to show how, in the context of Kerala, communist world-
making evolved between the early and mid-twentieth century,
underpinned by conﬂicting visions.
Second,
the
situated
nature
of
worldmaking
is
evident
throughout. Despite its seemingly abstract label, worldmaking is
always located in a particular context e reﬂecting historically and
geographically speciﬁc ways of knowing the world. As such, while
worldmaking can illuminate alternative or subaltern political pro-
jects, it can also serve to provincialise dominant discourses by
revealing their own constructed and contingent nature. Niamh
NicGhabhann Coleman, Zenobie Garrett and Frances Kane reﬂect
on their public engagement work with the archives of the ﬁrst
Ordnance Survey of Ireland. They show how one form of colonial
worldmaking can be repurposed to engage alternative narratives of
belonging and place. Likewise, Karolina Cwiek-Rogalska explores
the worldmaking practices of Polish settlers in the ‘Recovered
Territories’, the former eastern territories of Germany and the Free
City of Danzig that became part of Poland after World War II. Rachel
Dishington critically the archive of the Stevenson engineering ﬁrm.
In ways that echo Spivak's reading of worlding, Dishington shows
how archives shaped the terms of credible scientiﬁc knowledge. In
a similar vein, Apurba Chatterjee's work asks how William Jones's
botanical studies enacted an imperial practice of worldmaking that
translated expertise in the natural world to colonial power.
Third, the relationship between worldmaking and institution-
building is explored throughout. While worldmaking may appear a
predominantly intellectual outlook, the papers show how it ach-
ieves collective, concrete form through institutional structures.
Here, there is a clear overlap with the record-keeping and archiving
practices that are archetypical of organisations. Michelle Carmody,
for example, uses Amnesty International's Sierra Leonean section to
show how Eurocentric development was challenged from within
the organisation through appeals to decentralisation. Sarah Chant
turns a critical eye to the Alabama Department of Archives and
History, the ﬁrst government-funded independent archival insti-
tution in the United States, to demonstrate how this foundational
site of white worldmaking has faced conservative backlashes over
recent efforts to reconsider its collections. Olivia Mason's work
considers the role of the archive in constructing ideas of Jordanian
nationhood. Lara Lookabaugh and Banu G€okariksel's paper asks
how students' curricular work with a university collection might
enable projects of worldmaking.

within it. They also underscore the urgency of better understanding
the relationship between archives and worldmaking in the present
moment, prompting at least four areas for future research.
First, how do colonial logics endure in contemporary archiving
practices, and what possibilities for liberation might remain?
Eunsong Kim's work, for instance, encourages us to question
whether current archiving and commercial museum work, which is
increasingly enabled by ﬁnancial capital, perpetuate a form of
colonial worlding e akin to a colonial administrator imposing order
and value e rather than fostering inclusive worldmaking, even
when employing the language of reparation. This dynamic is starkly
evident in the digital realm, where companies like Adam Matthew
Digital acquire vast colonial archives and re-enclose them behind
paywalls. This practice effectively restricts access for scholars in
Global South institutions while co-opting the language of diversity
and inclusion. In this, they indicate continuities in the racial capi-
talist regimes that shape the creation of value from historical ma-
terials, turning them into legitimate archives. As Kim argues, the
enclosure of archival materials as objects with monetary value
shapes the construction of race, naturalising hierarchies through
the logic of capital.
Second, how can writing on archives engage with scholarship in
geography and beyond on racial capitalism, property, carcerality,
and enclosure? While many of the papers in this collection critically
examine archives as sites of colonial worldmaking, they prompt a
more substantial dialogue with geographies of racial capitalist
enclosure and carcerality. This further raises questions on the ter-
ritorialising effect of archives e how do archival materials shape the
articulation of national and imperial boundaries? Simultaneously,
this conversation might illuminate the potential for liberatory
worldmaking through practices such as counter-archiving, un-
archiving, archival refusal, and critical historical geographies of
collecting that actively challenge existing power structures and
create space for alternative futures.
Third, what is the role of archives in an age dominated by
misinformation? This question raises concerns about the ‘place’ of
archives in two senses: how is the value of archival, artefactual
evidence assessed, and where can it be safely stored, both physically
and digitally? The recent interest in archives has also brought greater
political intervention in the collection, management, and organisa-
tion of records. The rise of alternative archiving projects, from the
Invisible
Histories
project
to
the
Living
Refugee
Archive,
reﬂects concerns that the political and cultural memory of speciﬁc
groupsdespecially those marginalised by or critical of state-centric
practicesdare vulnerable targets for misinformation or deliberate
removal.21 This is particularly acute for groups that exist at the
margins of formal state systems: indigenous peoples, stateless na-
tions, racial and religious minorities.22 For example, the recent work
of the Unrepresented Peoples and Nations Organisation to safeguard
endangered archives of national and regional movements has shown
the growing threat posed by state-led misinformation campaigns.
Finally, what is the speciﬁc role of archivists and archival theory
in this expanded understanding of archives? One challenge posed
by the recent broadening of interest in archives is that the concept
has expanded to encompass a diverse range of approaches. For
some, the archive is primarily a metaphorical or discursive
construct, while for others, it refers to a tightly deﬁned set of
processes undertaken by trained professionals (such as curation,
cataloguing, and arrangement) and guided by core principles (like
respect des fonds and original order). Indeed, it is often these pro-
cesses that lend archives their accrediting functions e the methods
by which a historical object becomes an archival one. As scholar-
ship continues to explore a wide array of archiving and counter-
archiving practices, key questions remain: what connects these
diverse approaches? How can a closer dialogue between archivists
and geographers be fostered to bridge these different perspectives?
