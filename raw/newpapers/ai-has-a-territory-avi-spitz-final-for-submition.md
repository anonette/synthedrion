Introduction
Artificial intelligence is often discussed as if sovereignty over AI meant the ability of a state to regulate
algorithms, protect data, or develop national AI models. These dimensions are important, but they are not
sufficient. Frontier AI depends on infrastructures that are physical, expensive, energy-intensive, and often
privately owned: data centers, accelerators, electricity grids, cooling systems, optical networks, cloud contracts,
and security arrangements. AI is therefore not placeless. It has a territory.
This essay asks: what does AI sovereignty mean when the power to develop and govern artificial intelligence
depends on privately owned, energy-intensive, and globally distributed data-center infrastructure? The main
claim is that AI sovereignty cannot be understood only as control over models, data, or regulation. In the age of
frontier AI, sovereignty increasingly depends on control over compute infrastructure. Data centers are the
clearest example of this transformation because they turn AI into a territorial and geopolitical problem. They
reveal a tension between formal sovereignty and what may be called infrastructural suzerainty: a condition in
which states may claim authority over AI, while practical control remains with corporations, foreign chip
suppliers, cloud providers, energy infrastructures, or security arrangements.
Background: AI Sovereignty and the Geography of Compute
The recent wave of generative AI has changed the material scale of computation. Training and operating frontier
models requires specialized chips, continuous electricity, advanced cooling, high-speed networks, land, permits,
capital, and secure facilities. Data centers have therefore become central to the political economy of AI. The
International Energy Agency projects that global electricity consumption by data centers could more than double
to around 945 TWh by 2030 (International Energy Agency 2025). This projection matters because it shows that
AI is not merely digital. It is industrial, environmental, territorial, and geopolitical.
This territorial dimension is already visible in the changing geography of AI infrastructure. Large projects in the
Gulf show that AI compute is moving toward locations that can offer energy, land, capital, political support, and
strategic positioning. In the UAE, Stargate UAE in Abu Dhabi is planned as a major AI compute cluster, with its
first 200MW phase expected to begin operation in 2026 and a broader plan for 1GW of capacity (OpenAI 2025).
Saudi Arabia is also positioning itself as an AI infrastructure hub: AWS has announced a Saudi cloud region
planned for 2026, while HUMAIN, backed by the Public Investment Fund, is developing data-center capacity in
locations such as Riyadh and Dammam (Amazon Web Services 2024; Reuters 2025). These examples show that
AI sovereignty is increasingly tied to where compute can be built, powered, cooled, financed, and politically
secured.

At the same time, the geography of compute is contested locally. In the United States, communities increasingly
resist data centers because AI infrastructure arrives as electricity demand, water use, land conversion, cooling
systems, substations, noise, and zoning decisions. The Imperial Valley case, illustrated in Figure 5, makes this
concrete: after negotiations over recycled water stalled, Imperial Valley Computer Manufacturing sued the
Imperial Irrigation District for access to about 260 million gallons of Colorado River water per year, while local
reporting found that the project would require more electricity than Imperial County consumed in 2024
(McLean 2026).
The search for new compute territories is not limited to land. Recent proposals for floating data centers at sea
and orbital data centers in space show that AI infrastructure is pushing against terrestrial constraints: land
scarcity, electricity bottlenecks, cooling requirements, security risks, and local opposition (Hsu 2026; Sriram
and Roulette 2026). These proposals reveal that AI’s material demands are so intense that new geographies of
computation are being imagined.
Analysis
Data Centers as Infrastructure
Science and technology studies offers a useful language for understanding why data centers matter. Susan Leigh
Star argues that infrastructure is often invisible until it breaks, becomes scarce, or becomes contested (Star
1999). This applies directly to AI. When users interact with a chatbot, the data center disappears from view. The
model seems to live in an abstract cloud. But when electricity demand rises, cooling systems require water, local
communities protest land use, or governments restrict chips, the supposedly invisible infrastructure becomes
politically visible.
Star and Ruhleder also show that infrastructure is relational: it is embedded in practices, standards, institutions,
and communities (Star and Ruhleder 1996). A data center is therefore not just a building containing servers. It is
an arrangement that joins real estate, energy utilities, water systems, fiber networks, hardware supply chains,

maintenance labor, zoning law, finance, and national security. Its political meaning changes depending on who
depends on it, who pays for it, and who controls it.
Kate Crawford’s argument in Atlas of AI strengthens this point. Crawford describes AI as an extractive system
built from minerals, labor, data, and energy rather than as pure intelligence (Crawford 2021). Data centers make
this extraction concrete. They connect the abstract language of “AI capability” to the material realities of
electricity demand, cooling, land, emissions, chips, and labor. Data centers are not merely the background of AI.
They are part of the object called AI.
Sovereignty and Infrastructural Suzerainty
The concept of sovereignty usually refers to a state’s authority over territory, law, and political decision-making.
In digital policy, sovereignty is often discussed in relation to data localization, privacy regulation, national AI
strategies, or domestic language models. Data centers show that these are not enough. A country may regulate
AI outputs or require local data storage, but still depend on foreign infrastructure for the computation that makes
AI possible.
Recent work on compute sovereignty breaks the issue into layers: whether compute exists on a country’s
territory, who owns or operates that compute, and whose accelerators power it (Hawkins, Lehdonvirta, and Wu
2025). This layered approach is important because sovereignty is not binary. A country may have territorial
sovereignty without operational sovereignty. It may host AI infrastructure without controlling the cloud
platform. It may own data but lack the compute to train or deploy models at scale. It may announce a national
AI strategy while depending on foreign chips, foreign software stacks, and foreign cloud providers.
This is where the term infrastructural suzerainty becomes useful. Historically, suzerainty describes indirect
domination: one political entity formally governs itself, but another exercises decisive influence over its
strategic choices. In AI, a similar structure can emerge through infrastructure. A state may formally regulate AI
within its borders, but if data centers, chips, software stacks, cloud services, or security updates are controlled by
external corporations or foreign jurisdictions, then practical power lies elsewhere. The state has formal
sovereignty, while infrastructure creates dependence.

This distinction is crucial for AI geopolitics. Hosting a data center may look like national technological power.
But the deeper questions are different: who can decide how the infrastructure is used, upgraded, audited,
secured, or disconnected? Who controls emergency access? Which legal jurisdiction governs the provider? Who
can impose sanctions, export controls, licensing restrictions, or service interruptions? Who pays the
environmental and energy costs? AI sovereignty is therefore about operational control over the conditions of
computation.
Risk, Regulation, and New Territories of Compute
If data centers are the material base of AI power, they are also points of vulnerability. Once AI infrastructure
becomes essential for government, finance, logistics, research, military planning, and public services, data
centers begin to resemble strategic infrastructures such as ports, electricity grids, undersea cables, or energy
pipelines. CISA’s definition of critical infrastructure is useful here because it includes physical or virtual assets
whose disruption would affect security, economic security, public health, or safety (Cybersecurity and
Infrastructure Security Agency n.d.).
These risks are not limited to direct attack. Data centers depend on electricity, cooling, connectivity, access
control, supply chains, and continuous maintenance. Their operation can be shaped by grid shortages, export
controls, sanctions, cyber risks, corporate decisions, or geopolitical crisis. Infrastructural suzerainty therefore
operates not only through ownership, but also through dependence. The actor that controls chips, cloud services,
updates, security, or operational continuity may exercise power even without formal sovereignty.
AI regulation must therefore expand. It should not only ask what models are allowed to do. It should also ask
where computation takes place, who owns it, what energy systems support it, how cooling is managed, what
security standards apply, and how public costs are distributed. This does not mean that every data center should

be nationalized. It means that compute infrastructure should be treated as strategic infrastructure rather than
merely private real estate.
Conclusion
The central conclusion of this essay is that AI sovereignty should be tested less by political
declarations than by infrastructural capacity. A state may publish a national AI strategy, encourage
domestic models, or host a major data center, but these steps do not by themselves guarantee
sovereignty. The decisive question is whether it can secure the conditions that keep AI systems
operating: electricity, cooling, chips, cloud platforms, software access, security, maintenance, and
emergency continuity. Sovereignty, in this sense, is not only a legal status. It is an operational
capability.
This conclusion also changes how AI regulation should be imagined. Regulation cannot remain
focused only on model outputs, risk categories, privacy, or safety audits. These instruments address the
visible layer of AI, but not the infrastructure that determines who can build, run, interrupt, or scale it.
Future AI governance will therefore need to include compute planning, ownership transparency,
energy and water accountability, resilience standards, and crisis-access rules. Data centers should be
treated neither as ordinary private real estate nor as merely technical facilities, but as strategic
infrastructures that connect local resources to geopolitical power.
The future test of AI sovereignty will therefore be operational rather than declarative. States and publics will
have to ask whether they can govern the territory of computation: the water, electricity, land, cooling, chips,
cloud contracts, security protocols, and emergency access on which AI depends. Without such control,
sovereignty over AI remains a political aspiration rather than an infrastructural capacity.
